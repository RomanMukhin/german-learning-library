## Consonants

The Spanish alphabet (*el abecedario*) has 27 letters: the 26 of English plus *ñ*. The digraphs *ch*, *ll* and *rr* used to be listed separately and are still taught as single sounds. Most consonants sound close to English, so here we focus on the ones that differ.

| Letter | Name | How it sounds | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *b, v* | *be*, *uve* | identical: a firm *b* at the start, soft (lips barely touching) between vowels | *vaso*, *lobo* |
| *c* | *ce* | *k* before *a, o, u*; *s* (or *th* in much of Spain) before *e, i* | *casa*, *cena* |
| *ch* | *che* | like *ch* in *church* | *chico* |
| *d* | *de* | tongue on the teeth; soft *th* as in *then* between vowels | *dedo*, *nada* |
| *g* | *ge* | *g* in *go* before *a, o, u*; a throaty *h* before *e, i* | *gato*, *gente* |
| *h* | *hache* | always silent | *hola*, *ahora* |
| *j* | *jota* | a throaty *h*, like Scottish *loch* | *jamón* |
| *ll* | *elle* | like *y* in *yes* in most places | *llave* |
| *ñ* | *eñe* | like *ny* in *canyon* | *año* |
| *qu* | *cu* | always *k*; the *u* is silent | *queso* |
| *r* | *erre* | a single tap (like *tt* in American *butter*); trilled at the start of a word | *pero*, *rojo* |
| *rr* | *doble erre* | a strong trill | *perro* |
| *z* | *zeta* | *s* in Latin America, *th* in much of Spain | *zapato* |
| *x* | *equis* | *ks*; in some Mexican names a throaty *h* | *examen*, *México* |
| *y* | *ye* | *y* in *yes*; like *i* alone or at the end | *yo*, *hoy* |

### Silent u after g and q

To keep a hard *g* before *e* or *i*, Spanish writes *gu*: *guerra*, *guitarra* — the *u* is silent. To pronounce it, add a dieresis: *pingüino*, *vergüenza*.

> [!trap] Pero vs perro
> One tap or a trill changes the word: *pero* (but) vs *perro* (dog), *caro* (expensive) vs *carro* (car).

## Vowels

Spanish has only **five** vowel sounds, and each one is short, clear and always pronounced the same way, whether stressed or not. There is no "uh" sound like the *a* in English *about*.

| Vowel | Sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *a* | *a* in *father*, but shorter | *casa* |
| *e* | *e* in *bed* | *mesa* |
| *i* | *ee* in *see*, but shorter | *sí* |
| *o* | *o* in *note*, without the glide to *u* | *todo* |
| *u* | *oo* in *food* | *luna* |

» *Ana* is always *AH-nah*, never "Anna" with a weak second vowel.
» *chocolate* is *cho-co-LA-te*, four clear syllables, with a full *e* at the end.

### Strong and weak vowels

*a, e, o* are **strong** vowels; *i* (and *y* at the end of a word) and *u* are **weak**. A weak vowel next to another vowel glides into it to form a **diphthong**, which counts as one syllable: *bien*, *agua*, *ciudad*, *hoy*. Two strong vowels side by side stay separate: *le-er*, *ca-er*, *po-e-ma*.

> [!hack] Don't glide
> English vowels tend to slide (*no* sounds like "noh-oo"). Keep Spanish vowels pure and steady: *no* is just *noh*.

## Syllables

Knowing how a word divides into syllables helps you pronounce it and tells you where to put accents. The main rules:

### One consonant between vowels

It goes with the following vowel: *ca-sa*, *pe-lo*, *mo-ne-da*.

### Two consonants between vowels

They are usually split: *ar-te*, *can-tar*, *tiem-po*. But a consonant + *l* or *r* (*bl, br, cl, cr, dr, fl, fr, gl, gr, pl, pr, tr*) stays together and starts the next syllable: *ha-blar*, *li-bro*, *o-tro*. The digraphs *ch*, *ll* and *rr* are never split: *co-che*, *ca-lle*, *pe-rro*.

### Three or more consonants

The last one (or the last pair if it's one of the groups above) goes with the next vowel: *ins-tan-te*, *com-pra*, *nom-bre*.

### Vowels

A diphthong stays in one syllable: *sie-te*, *nue-vo*, *bai-le*. Two strong vowels split: *te-a-tro*, *ma-es-tro*. A weak vowel with a written accent breaks the diphthong: *dí-a*, *pa-ís*, *ba-úl*.

» *Mur-cié-la-go* — bat (4 syllables)
» ~~*a-le-gr-í-a*~~ → *a-le-grí-a* — joy (4 syllables)

> [!fact] Clapping practice
> Clap once per syllable as you say a word. Spanish rhythm is "syllable-timed": every syllable takes about the same time, which is why it sounds fast to English ears.

## Word Stress

Every Spanish word of two or more syllables has one syllable that is said louder and longer. Where it falls follows simple rules, so you can pronounce almost any word you read.

### Rule 1: ends in a vowel, -n or -s → stress on the second-to-last syllable

» *ca*-sa, *ha*-blan, *li*-bros, *jo*-ven, ca-*mi*-sa

### Rule 2: ends in any other consonant → stress on the last syllable

» ha-*blar*, ciu-*dad*, pa-*pel*, re-*loj*, fe-*liz*

### Exceptions get a written accent

If a word doesn't follow those two rules, a written accent (*tilde*) marks the stressed syllable:

» *ca-fé* (ends in a vowel but stressed on the last syllable)
» *ár-bol* (ends in *-l* but stressed on the second-to-last)
» *mú-si-ca* (stress three syllables from the end — always accented)

### Stress changes meaning

» *papa* (potato / pope) vs *papá* (dad)
» *término* (term) vs *termino* (I finish) vs *terminó* (he finished)

> [!hack] Read, don't guess
> No accent? Apply rules 1 and 2. Accent? Stress that syllable. That's the whole system, and it never fails.

## Written Accents

The written accent (*la tilde*, an acute accent ´) has two jobs: it marks irregular stress, and it tells apart words that are spelled alike.

### 1. Irregular stress

Words are grouped by where their stress falls:

| Type | Stress on | Written accent when... | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| *aguda* | last syllable | it ends in a vowel, *-n*, *-s* | *sofá*, *canción*, *además* |
| *llana* | second-to-last | it ends in any other consonant | *fácil*, *lápiz*, *azúcar* |
| *esdrújula* | third-to-last | always | *teléfono*, *sábado*, *rápido* |

### 2. Breaking a diphthong

An accent on *i* or *u* next to a strong vowel splits them into two syllables: *tío* (*tí-o*), *Raúl* (*Ra-úl*), *país*.

### 3. Telling one-syllable words apart (*tilde diacrítica*)

| With accent | Without accent |
|---|---|
| *él* — he | *el* — the |
| *tú* — you | *tu* — your |
| *mí* — me (after a preposition) | *mi* — my |
| *sí* — yes | *si* — if |
| *sé* — I know | *se* — reflexive pronoun |
| *más* — more | *mas* — but (literary) |
| *té* — tea | *te* — you (object) |

### 4. Question and exclamation words

*qué, quién, cómo, dónde, cuándo, cuánto, cuál* carry an accent when they ask or exclaim: *¿Dónde vives?* but *La casa donde vivo.* See [Asking Questions](beginner/asking-questions).

> [!trap] Capital letters keep their accents
> *Álvaro*, *ÉL*, *ÁFRICA* — accents are required on capitals too.

## Comprehensive Review 1

Pronunciation checklist:

» Five pure vowels: *a, e, i, o, u* — never reduced.
» *h* is silent: *hotel* = *otel*.
» *b* and *v* sound the same.
» *j* and *g* before *e/i* sound like a throaty *h*: *jefe*, *gente*.
» *ll* and *y* usually sound like English *y*.
» *ñ* = *ny*: *España*, *mañana*.
» *r* taps, *rr* (and initial *r*) trills.

### Practice: say these aloud

» *guitarra* — gui-*ta*-rra (silent *u*, trilled *rr*)
» *hijo* — *i*-jo (silent *h*, throaty *j*)
» *cereza* — ce-*re*-za (*c* and *z* as *s* or *th*)
» *llueve* — *llue*-ve (*ll* as *y*, diphthong *ue*)

## Comprehensive Review 2

### Practice: divide and find the stress

» *ventana* → ven-*ta*-na (ends in a vowel → second-to-last)
» *ordenador* → or-de-na-*dor* (ends in *-r* → last)
» *lámpara* → *lám*-pa-ra (third-to-last → accent)
» *camión* → ca-*mión* (ends in *-n*, stressed last → accent)
» *Rodríguez* → Ro-*drí*-guez (ends in *-z*, stressed second-to-last → accent)

### Accent or not?

» *Si quieres, ven.* — If you want, come. (*si* = if)
» *Sí, quiero.* — Yes, I want to. (*sí* = yes)
» *Este libro es para mí.* — This book is for me.
» *Mi libro es azul.* — My book is blue.

> [!hack] Spot the stress, earn the spelling
> Once you can hear the stressed syllable, written accents become logical rather than random: you add one only where the rules would point to the wrong syllable.
