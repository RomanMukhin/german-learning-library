## Impersonal Se

The **impersonal se** makes a general statement about what **people in general** do — what English expresses with *one*, *you*, *they* or *people*. No specific person is meant.

**Formula:** *se* + verb in the **third person singular**

» *Se vive bien en esta ciudad.* — People live well in this city. / Life is good here.
» *¿Cómo se dice "spoon" en español?* — How do you say "spoon" in Spanish?
» *Aquí no se fuma.* — You can't smoke here.
» *Se trabaja mucho en esta empresa.* — People work hard at this company.
» *¿Por dónde se va a la estación?* — How do you get to the station?

### When to use it

- Customs and general truths: *En España se cena tarde.*
- Rules and instructions: *No se puede aparcar aquí.*
- Asking for directions or how to do something: *¿Cómo se llega al museo?*

### What it combines with

The impersonal *se* is used with verbs that have **no direct object** (intransitive verbs like *vivir*, *ir*, *trabajar*), or with an object that is a **person introduced by *a***.

» *Se ayuda a los vecinos.* — People help their neighbours.
» *Se respeta a los mayores.* — Older people are respected.

Because there's no grammatical subject, the verb stays **singular**, even with plural people: *Se busca a los culpables* (They are looking for the culprits).

> [!trap] No reflexive verbs
> Verbs that already use *se* (like *levantarse*) can't take another *se*. Use *uno* instead: ~~Se se levanta temprano~~ → *Uno se levanta temprano* (One gets up early).

## Passive Se

The **passive se** says that something **is done** to a thing, without saying who does it. It's the most common way of expressing the passive in everyday Spanish.

**Formula:** *se* + third person verb (**singular or plural**) + noun

The verb **agrees** with the noun, because that noun is the grammatical subject.

» *Se vende piso.* — Flat for sale. (literally: a flat is sold)
» *Se venden pisos.* — Flats for sale.
» *Se habla inglés.* — English spoken.
» *Aquí se reparan bicicletas.* — Bicycles repaired here.
» *Se necesitan camareros.* — Waiters needed.

### Key points

- Only **transitive** verbs (verbs that can take a direct object) work: *vender*, *alquilar*, *buscar*, *hacer*.
- It's used mainly with **things**, not people.
- The agent (who does it) is **not** mentioned. ~~Se vendió la casa por mi padre~~ is wrong; use the *ser* passive or the active instead.

» *Se publicaron los resultados ayer.* — The results were published yesterday.
» *Se ha cancelado el vuelo.* — The flight has been cancelled.

> [!hack] Sign Spanish
> Signs and adverts love the passive *se*: *Se alquila* (to let), *Se prohíbe fumar* (no smoking), *Se aceptan tarjetas* (cards accepted).

## Impersonal vs. Passive Se

The two look identical in the singular, so the question is: **is there a thing that the verb agrees with?**

| | Impersonal se | Passive se |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | people in general do something | something is done |
| Verb | always 3rd person singular | singular or plural, agrees with the noun |
| Verb type | intransitive, or + *a* + person | transitive, with a thing |
| Example | *Se come bien aquí.* | *Se comen muchas tapas aquí.* |

### Test: make it plural

Swap in a plural noun. If the verb must change, it's passive *se*.

» *Se vende el coche.* → *Se venden los coches.* — passive (verb agrees)
» *Se habla de política.* → *Se habla de las elecciones.* — impersonal (*de* + noun is not a subject, so no change)

### People: use the impersonal

With people as the object, the passive *se* would be ambiguous (it could sound reflexive: *se buscan* = they look for each other). So Spanish uses the impersonal *se* + *a*:

» *Se busca a los testigos.* — The witnesses are being sought.
» *Se contrató a dos profesoras.* — Two teachers were hired.

> [!fact] Usage in practice
> Without *a*, many speakers use agreement with people when they're unspecific: *Se necesitan profesores* (Teachers wanted). With specific people and *a*, keep the verb singular.

## Comprehensive Review 1

Focus: forming impersonal *se* sentences.

Rewrite each sentence with impersonal *se*.

1. La gente come mucho pescado en Galicia.
2. En esta oficina uno trabaja hasta tarde.
3. No puedes entrar con perros.
4. ¿Cómo llegas al centro?
5. La gente vive mejor en el campo.

> [!fact] Answers
> 1. *Se come mucho pescado en Galicia.* (passive reading also possible) 2. *En esta oficina se trabaja hasta tarde.* 3. *No se puede entrar con perros.* 4. *¿Cómo se llega al centro?* 5. *Se vive mejor en el campo.*

> [!hack] Uno, la gente, tú
> These are your alternatives when *se* doesn't fit: *uno* (one), *la gente* + singular verb (people), and the generic *tú* in casual speech.

## Comprehensive Review 2

Focus: passive *se* and agreement.

Choose the correct verb.

1. Se (alquila / alquilan) habitaciones.
2. Se (busca / buscan) un electricista.
3. En el mercado se (vende / venden) frutas tropicales.
4. Se (prohíbe / prohíben) las fotos.
5. Se (necesita / necesitan) paciencia.

> [!fact] Answers
> 1. *alquilan* 2. *busca* 3. *venden* 4. *prohíben* 5. *necesita*

### With infinitives

If what follows is an infinitive, the verb stays singular: *Se prohíbe tomar fotos* (it's prohibited to take photos), but *Se prohíben las fotos*.

## Comprehensive Review 3

Focus: telling them apart. Label each sentence **I** (impersonal) or **P** (passive).

1. Se duerme muy bien en este hotel.
2. Se construyeron dos hospitales.
3. Se felicitó a los ganadores.
4. Se dice que va a nevar.
5. Se sirve el desayuno a las ocho.

> [!fact] Answers
> 1. I (no object) 2. P (*hospitales* agrees) 3. I (*a* + people) 4. I (*se dice que* = people say that) 5. P (*el desayuno* is the subject)

> [!fact] Se dice que
> *Se dice que*, *se cree que*, *se sabe que* are fixed impersonal openers: "it's said that", "it's believed that", "it's known that".

> [!trap] Drop the doer
> Once you use passive *se*, the agent disappears. If you need to say who did it, use the active sentence or the *ser* passive with *por*.
