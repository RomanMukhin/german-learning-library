Adverbs

Words that say where, when, how and why: the types of adverbs, where they stand in the sentence, and how to compare them.

An adverb adds information to a verb, an adjective, another adverb or a whole sentence: Sie singt schön, Das ist sehr gut, Leider regnet es. The great news for learners: German adverbs never take endings. No cases, no genders, no -ly. The work is in choosing the right word and putting it in the right place.

Adverb or adjective?

English marks most adverbs with -ly (quick → quickly). German does not: the bare adjective doubles as an adverb. You can only tell the two apart by what they describe.

Describes Ending? Example
Adjective before a noun a noun yes ein schnelles Auto — a fast car
Adjective after sein/werden the subject no Das Auto ist schnell. — The car is fast.
Adverb the verb / an adjective no Er fährt schnell. — He drives fast. / sehr schnell

Besides these "adjective adverbs", German has many words that are only adverbs and can never stand before a noun with an ending: hier, gestern, oft, gern, leider, sehr, deshalb.

The types at a glance

Type Question Examples
Place and direction wo? wohin? woher? hier, dort, oben, draußen, überall, dorthin, hierher, hinein
Time and frequency wann? wie oft? wie lange? jetzt, heute, bald, schon, immer, oft, nie, morgens
Manner and degree wie? wie sehr? gern, so, anders, zusammen, sehr, ziemlich, fast, kaum
Comment (sentence adverbs) what does the speaker think? leider, hoffentlich, vielleicht, wahrscheinlich, zum Glück
Reason and consequence warum? mit welcher Folge? deshalb, darum, also, trotzdem, sonst
Pronominal adverbs stand for "preposition + it/what" damit, darauf, dafür, womit, worauf, wofür

Where they go, how they compare

  • An adverb can open the sentence; then the verb comes second and the subject follows: Morgen fahre ich nach Wien. More on Position of adverbs.
  • In the middle of the sentence, the usual order is time – cause – manner – place: Ich fahre morgen wegen der Arbeit mit dem Zug nach Wien.
  • Adverbs from adjectives compare like adjectives, with am ... -sten in the superlative: schnell, schneller, am schnellsten. A few true adverbs have irregular forms: gern, lieber, am liebsten. See Comparison of adverbs.

Never between subject and verb

English puts many adverbs between subject and verb: "I often drink tea", "She always comes late". German never does in a main clause, because the verb must be second: Ich trinke oft Tee. Sie kommt immer zu spät. If you want the adverb early, put it in position 1: Oft trinke ich Tee.

Where did the -ly go?

Old High German had an adverb ending -o: skōni "beautiful" (adjective) but skōno "beautifully" (adverb). When unstressed vowels weakened to -e and then vanished, the two forms fell together, and German simply stopped marking adverbs. A trace survives in schon ("already"), which was once the adverb of schön. English went the other way and built a new ending from -lich ("like"), which became -ly.
