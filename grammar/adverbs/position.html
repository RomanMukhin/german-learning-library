Position of adverbs

Adverb in position 1 and the verb jumps forward, the middle field order time–cause–manner–place, adverbs around pronouns and objects, and nur, auch, sogar.

German adverbs are flexible: the same sentence can start with the subject or with an adverb, and in the middle there are a few slots where adverbs like to sit. But two rules are not flexible at all: the conjugated verb stays in second position in a main clause, and it goes to the end in a subordinate clause. Everything on this page fits around those two fixed points.

A2 core

Position 1: the adverb first, then the verb

Any adverb can open a main clause, usually to set the scene or for emphasis. The verb still comes second, so the subject moves behind the verb:

Position 1 Verb Subject Rest
Ichfahre–morgen nach Wien.
Morgenfahreichnach Wien.
Leiderhatesden ganzen Tag geregnet.
Deshalbbleibenwirzu Hause.
Hierdarfmannicht parken.

Not "Morgen ich fahre"

In English the subject stays before the verb even after an adverb: Tomorrow I'm going to Vienna. In German that would be two elements before the verb, which is not allowed: Morgen ich fahre nach Wien. Correct: Morgen fahre ich nach Wien.

After the verb, not before it

When the subject comes first, the adverb goes after the conjugated verb, never between subject and verb as in English:

  • Ich trinke oft Kaffee. — I often drink coffee. (not Ich oft trinke)
  • Sie kommt immer zu spät. — She always comes late.
  • Wir haben gestern lange gearbeitet. — We worked late yesterday.

In subordinate clauses

In a clause with weil, dass, wenn, ob etc. the verb is at the end, and the adverb sits in the middle, usually right after the subject: ..., weil ich morgen nach Wien fahre. / Ich weiß, dass er oft zu spät kommt.

B1

Several adverbs: time – cause – manner – place

When several adverbs or adverbial phrases meet in the middle of the sentence, the neutral order is Te-Ka-Mo-Lo: temporal (when?) – kausal (why?) – modal (how?) – lokal (where?).

Subject + verb Time Cause Manner Place End
Ich fahremorgenwegen eines Terminsmit dem Zugnach Wien.
Sie istgestern–sehr schnellnach Hausegelaufen.
Wir treffen unsheute Abend––im Café.

The short version that solves most cases: time before place. Ich bin heute hier, not Ich bin hier heute (the English order "I'm here today"). Detailed rules for the whole middle field are on Word order.

TeKaMoLo, or just "wann – warum – wie – wo"

Ask the questions in alphabetical order of the German words: wann? warum? wie? wo? That is exactly the order the answers go in.

Adverbs and objects

The position of an adverb relative to the objects depends on whether the object is "old" (known) or "new" information:

  • Pronoun objects come straight after the verb, before the adverb: Ich habe ihn gestern gesehen. (not Ich habe gestern ihn gesehen)
  • A dative noun or a definite noun (known thing) usually stands before a manner or place adverb: Ich habe das Buch schnell gelesen. Time adverbs can come before or after it: Ich habe gestern meiner Mutter geholfen / meiner Mutter gestern geholfen.
  • An indefinite noun (new information) comes after the adverbs: Ich habe gestern ein Buch gekauft.
  • A reflexive pronoun is a pronoun too, so the adverb follows it: Wir treffen uns heute.

Adverbs that focus: nur, auch, sogar, erst, noch

Focus adverbs stand directly before the word they refer to, and moving them changes the meaning:

  • Nur Anna hat mir geholfen. — Only Anna helped me (nobody else).
  • Anna hat nur mir geholfen. — Anna helped only me (no one else).
  • Anna hat mir nur geholfen. — Anna only helped me (she didn't do it for me).
  • Sogar mein Opa hat ein Smartphone. — Even my grandpa has a smartphone.
  • Ich komme auch. / Auch ich komme. — I'm coming too.

Adverbs inside a noun phrase or before an adjective

Degree adverbs go directly before the adjective or adverb they modify: ein sehr schönes Haus, ziemlich spät, viel zu teuer. Place and time adverbs can follow a noun like a short description: das Haus dort (that house over there), die Zeitung von gestern, der Mann da.

Classic mistakes

  • Heute ich habe keine Zeit → Heute habe ich keine Zeit.
  • Ich immer trinke Tee → Ich trinke immer Tee.
  • Ich war dort gestern → Ich war gestern dort. Time before place.
  • Ich habe gestern ihn getroffen → Ich habe ihn gestern getroffen. Pronouns first.
  • ..., weil ich habe heute keine Zeit → ..., weil ich heute keine Zeit habe.

Position 1 is the spotlight

Only one element fits before the verb, so whatever stands there gets the spotlight. Starting with the adverb is not just correct, it changes the focus: Ich gehe morgen ins Kino is neutral, Morgen gehe ich ins Kino stresses "tomorrow (not today)". German speakers start sentences with adverbs far more often than English speakers do, so doing it makes you sound more natural.

Later: where nicht goes

nicht is an adverb too, and its position follows similar logic: before the element it negates, after pronoun and definite objects, usually after time adverbs: Ich habe ihn gestern nicht gesehen. See Negation.
