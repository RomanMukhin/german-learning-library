Relative pronouns

der, die, das, denen, dessen and deren in relative clauses, plus prepositions, was, wo and wer.

A relative clause describes a noun: "the man who lives next door", "the book that I bought". In German it starts with a relative pronoun, which is almost always a form of der / die / das, and it sends the verb to the end. English can often drop "who/that" (the book I bought); German never can.

It is the definite article with four exceptions: dative plural denen and the genitive forms dessen / deren. These are the same forms as the demonstrative pronoun (see Demonstrative pronouns).

Two questions, two answers

Choosing the form is a two-step job, and each step looks in a different place:

  1. Gender and number come from the noun in the main clause that you describe.
  2. Case comes from the job of the pronoun inside the relative clause: subject, accusative object, dative object, after a preposition, owner.
  • Der Mann, der neben mir wohnt, ist Arzt. — The man who lives next to me is a doctor. (masc., subject → der)
  • Der Mann, den ich gestern getroffen habe, ist Arzt. — The man (whom) I met yesterday is a doctor. (masc., object of treffen → den)
  • Der Mann, dem ich geholfen habe, ist Arzt. — The man I helped is a doctor. (masc., helfen + dative → dem)
  • Die Kollegin, die ich anrufe, heißt Eva. — The colleague I'm calling is called Eva.
  • Das Buch, das auf dem Tisch liegt, gehört mir. — The book that is on the table is mine.
  • Die Leute, denen das Haus gehört, wohnen in München. — The people who own the house live in Munich. (plural, gehören + dative → denen)

Split it into two sentences

Rewrite the relative clause as a sentence of its own, with the article: Ich habe den Mann gestern getroffen. The article you get (den) is your relative pronoun: der Mann, den ich gestern getroffen habe. Only in the dative plural and genitive do you need the long forms.

Commas and word order

A relative clause is a subordinate clause: always set off with commas on both sides, and the conjugated verb goes to the end. It usually stands right after the noun it describes, which can split the main clause in two:

  • Die Wohnung, die wir gemietet haben, ist sehr hell. — The flat we rented is very bright.
  • Ich habe die Wohnung gesehen, die du mir empfohlen hast. — I saw the flat you recommended to me. (when only the participle would be left after it, the relative clause can move behind the end of the main clause)

With prepositions

If the verb inside the relative clause needs a preposition, the preposition goes at the very start of the clause, before the pronoun, and it decides the case. English often puts the preposition at the end (the friend I went with); German never does.

  • Das ist die Freundin, mit der ich in Urlaub fahre. — That's the friend I'm going on holiday with. (mit + dative fem.)
  • Der Film, über den alle sprechen, läuft im Kino. — The film everyone is talking about is on at the cinema. (sprechen über + accusative)
  • Die Kinder, mit denen mein Sohn spielt, sind sehr nett. — The children my son plays with are very nice.
  • Das ist das Problem, an das ich die ganze Zeit denke. — That's the problem I keep thinking about.

dessen and deren: whose

For "whose", use dessen (masculine and neuter noun in the main clause) or deren (feminine or plural). The form follows the owner, not the thing owned, and the noun after it takes no article:

  • Der Nachbar, dessen Hund immer bellt, ist ausgezogen. — The neighbour whose dog always barks has moved out.
  • Die Frau, deren Auto vor der Tür steht, ist meine Chefin. — The woman whose car is outside is my boss.
  • Das Kind, dessen Eltern verreist sind, wohnt bei uns. — The child whose parents are away is staying with us.
  • Meine Freunde, deren Tochter in Wien studiert, ... — My friends, whose daughter studies in Vienna, ...

Classic mistakes

  • Die Leute, mit den ich arbeite → mit denen ich arbeite. Dative plural is denen.
  • Der Film, der ich gesehen habe → den ich gesehen habe. Inside the clause it is the object.
  • Das Buch, ich gekauft habe → Das Buch, das ich gekauft habe. The pronoun can never be dropped.
  • Die Frau, dessen Sohn ... → deren Sohn. The owner (die Frau) decides, not the son.
  • Der Kollege, den ich mit arbeite → mit dem ich arbeite. The preposition comes first.
  • das (relative pronoun, one s) vs dass (conjunction "that"): das Haus, das ich kaufe / Ich weiß, dass du kommst.

B1+

was after alles, nichts, etwas, das

After indefinite words that do not name a noun, the relative pronoun is was:

  • Alles, was du sagst, stimmt. — Everything you say is true.
  • Es gibt nichts, was ich lieber esse. — There's nothing I'd rather eat.
  • Ich habe etwas gefunden, was dir gefallen wird. — I've found something you'll like.
  • Das, was er erzählt hat, war interessant. — What he told us was interesting.
  • Das ist das Beste, was ich je gegessen habe. — That's the best thing I've ever eaten. (after a superlative used as a noun)

was also refers to a whole clause: Er hat die Prüfung bestanden, was mich sehr freut ("He passed the exam, which makes me very happy"). With a preposition, this becomes wo(r)-: ..., worüber ich mich sehr freue.

wo for places (and times)

After a place, wo can replace in dem / in der; after a city or country name it is the only natural choice. woher and wohin work the same way:

  • Das ist das Café, wo (= in dem) wir uns kennengelernt haben. — That's the café where we met.
  • Ich fahre nach Graz, wo meine Oma wohnt. — I'm going to Graz, where my grandma lives.
  • Das Dorf, woher er kommt, ist winzig. — The village he comes from is tiny.

In speech wo is also used after time words: an dem Tag, wo wir uns getroffen haben. In careful writing prefer an dem or als.

wer and was without a noun: whoever, whatever

A relative clause can also start a sentence without any noun before it. Then wer means "whoever, anyone who" and was "whatever, what":

  • Wer zu spät kommt, muss draußen warten. — Anyone who comes late has to wait outside.
  • Was du heute kannst besorgen, das verschiebe nicht auf morgen. — What you can get done today, don't put off until tomorrow. (a proverb)

welcher in formal writing

Instead of der/die/das you may read welcher / welche / welches in official or old-fashioned texts, mostly to avoid a repetition like die, die: Die Frau, welche die Firma leitet, .... There is no genitive form; use dessen/deren. In speech, simply use der/die/das.

"which" for a whole idea is was

English sometimes uses "which" for a whole idea: "He came late, which annoyed me." Learners then write ..., das mich geärgert hat. But das needs a neuter noun to point back to. For a whole idea German uses was: Er kam zu spät, was mich geärgert hat.

Later: more advanced patterns

Relative clauses with genitive prepositions (der Grund, wegen dessen ...), long genitive chains and the participle as an alternative to a relative clause are on the relative pronouns reference.
