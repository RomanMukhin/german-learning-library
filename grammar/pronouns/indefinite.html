Indefinite pronouns

man, jemand, niemand, etwas, nichts, alle, jeder, einige and the irgend- words for people and things you do not name.

Indefinite pronouns talk about people or things without naming them: "somebody", "nothing", "everyone", "one". German uses them constantly, especially man, which is the everyday way to make general statements. This page covers the forms you need for A2 and B1 conversation.

A2 core

German English Opposite
man one, people, you –
jemand somebody niemand (nobody)
etwas / was something nichts (nothing)
alle all, everybody (plural) keiner
alles everything nichts
jeder / jede / jedes each, every keiner

Examples

  • Hier darf man nicht rauchen. – You can't smoke here.
  • Jemand hat angerufen. – Somebody called.
  • Niemand weiß das. – Nobody knows that.
  • Möchtest du etwas trinken? – Would you like something to drink?
  • Ich habe nichts gesehen. – I didn't see anything.
  • Alle sind da. – Everybody is here. (plural verb)
  • Alles ist gut. – Everything is fine. (singular verb)
  • Jeder Mensch ist anders. – Every person is different.

man = the general "you"

Whenever English uses a general "you", "one" or "people" (people say, you don't do that, one can), German usually uses man + 3rd person singular: Man sagt, Das macht man nicht, Man kann. One verb form, no need to think about who.

man is not Mann

man (one n, lowercase) = "one/people". der Mann (two n, capital) = "the man". Man sagt is a general statement; Der Mann sagt is a specific man speaking.

B1

man in other cases: einen, einem

man only exists in the nominative. In the accusative and dative German borrows from ein:

  • Das Wetter kann einen müde machen. – The weather can make you tired.
  • Das kann einem passieren. – That can happen to anyone.
  • Wenn man müde ist, hilft einem Kaffee. – When you're tired, coffee helps you.

And the possessive of man is sein: Man muss seine Hausaufgaben machen.

jemand / niemand endings

In speech jemand and niemand often have no ending. In writing you will usually see accusative jemanden/niemanden and dative jemandem/niemandem: Ich habe niemanden gesehen. Ich habe es niemandem erzählt. With etwas/nichts there is never an ending.

etwas / nichts + adjective

After etwas, nichts, viel, wenig an adjective becomes a capitalised noun with -es: etwas Neues (something new), nichts Besonderes (nothing special), viel Gutes. Exception: anders → etwas anderes, which is usually lowercase.

einige, manche, mehrere, viele

All four go with plural nouns and have the die-plural endings:

  • einige Leute – some/a few people
  • manche Leute – some people (but not others)
  • mehrere Leute – several people (more than two)
  • viele Leute – many people

Manche Leute mögen keinen Kaffee = some people, in contrast to the others. Einige Leute waren schon da = a few people.

jeder and alle

jeder is singular and takes article endings: jeder Tag, jede Woche, jedes Jahr, mit jedem Kind. alle is plural: alle Tage, alle Kinder. So: Jeder kommt (singular verb), Alle kommen (plural verb).

beide and beides

beide means "both" (two people or countable things) and takes a plural verb. beides sums up two different things or ideas and takes a singular verb:

  • Ich habe Tom und Mia gefragt. Beide kommen mit. — I asked Tom and Mia. Both are coming along.
  • Ich kenne beide. Mit beiden war ich in der Schule. — I know both of them. I went to school with both.
  • Tee oder Kaffee? – Beides ist gut. — Tea or coffee? – Either is fine.
  • Meine beiden Schwestern wohnen in Wien. — Both my sisters live in Vienna. (after an article it takes adjective endings)

einiges, manches, vieles: some of it

In the plural, einige, manche, viele count people or things. The neuter singular forms einiges, manches, vieles, weniges mean "a certain amount of something", "some of it", and take a singular verb: Einiges war neu für mich ("Some of it was new to me"), Ich habe vieles gelernt ("I learned a lot").

einer, keiner, welche

The words ein and kein can stand alone as pronouns. They then take the full endings: einer / eine / eins ("one") and keiner / keine / keins ("none, nobody"). For "some" with uncountable or plural nouns German uses welche:

  • Hast du einen Stift? – Ja, ich habe einen. / Nein, ich habe keinen. — Do you have a pen? – Yes, I have one. / No, I don't have one.
  • Einer meiner Kollegen spricht Ukrainisch. — One of my colleagues speaks Ukrainian.
  • Keiner hat mir geholfen. — Nobody helped me. (spoken alternative to niemand)
  • Wir brauchen Milch. – Im Kühlschrank ist noch welche. — We need milk. – There's still some in the fridge.
  • Hast du Äpfel? – Ja, ich habe welche gekauft. — Do you have apples? – Yes, I bought some.

Genitive forms exist only in the plural and are formal: die Meinung aller ("everyone's opinion").

Referring back to an indefinite pronoun

When you come back to an indefinite pronoun later in the sentence, pick the matching personal, possessive or relative word:

Indefinite Refer back with Example
jemand, niemand, jederer, sein, derJeder, der mitkommen will, muss seinen Pass mitbringen.
manman, sein (no relative)Wenn man müde ist, sollte man schlafen. (not er)
alles, nichts, etwas, einiges, vieleses, wasAlles, was du brauchst, ist hier.
alle, beide, einige, manchesie, ihr, dieAlle, die gekommen sind, haben ihre Kinder mitgebracht.

With etwas both das and was are heard as relative pronouns; was is the safer choice: etwas, was mir gefällt.

irgend- words

irgend- adds "some... or other, I don't know/care which":

Word Meaning
irgendwo somewhere
irgendwann sometime
irgendwie somehow
irgendwer / irgendjemand someone or other
irgendwas / irgendetwas something or other
irgendein / irgendeine some ... (any)
  • Ich habe meine Brille irgendwo liegen lassen. – I've left my glasses somewhere.
  • Wir sehen uns irgendwann. – We'll see each other sometime.
  • Gib mir irgendein Buch. – Give me any book.

irgend- = "some-"

English adds some- (somewhere, sometime, somehow, someone); German adds irgend- in front: irgendwo, irgendwann, irgendwie, irgendwer. Same idea.

man, the old "man"

man comes from the same word as Mann: in Old High German man meant "human being, person". The general pronoun works like English one, and it developed just like French on, which comes from Latin homo ("human").

Later: wer, was as indefinites

In speech wer and was also mean "someone/something": Ist da wer? (Is anyone there?), Ich hab was gehört (I heard something). Common, but informal.
