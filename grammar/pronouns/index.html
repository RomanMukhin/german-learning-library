Pronouns

The small words that stand in for nouns: personal, possessive, indefinite and demonstrative pronouns.

Pronouns save you from repeating nouns. German pronouns change with case (nominative, accusative, dative) and often with gender, so they are one of the best places to practise the case system without thinking about a whole noun phrase.

Which one when

  • You know who it is and just want to avoid repeating the noun: personal pronoun. Ich sehe den Film. → Ich sehe ihn.
  • You want to say whose it is: possessive. Das ist mein Auto.
  • You do not know or do not care who: indefinite. Man sagt, ... / Jemand hat angerufen.
  • You want to point or stress: demonstrative. Der da? Der ist nett.

All four families share the same logic: the ending tells you the case. Once you know der → den → dem and er → ihn → ihm, most pronouns fall into place.

The r-n-m ladder

The masculine endings run -r (nominative), -n (accusative), -m (dative): der/den/dem, er/ihn/ihm, dieser/diesen/diesem, meiner/meinen/meinem. If you are unsure about a masculine pronoun, climb the ladder: who does it? -r. Whom? -n. To whom? -m.

Why "Sie" is a capitalised "they"

The polite Sie is grammatically the third person plural sie ("they"). In the 17th–18th centuries addressing someone indirectly in the plural was considered the most respectful option, and it won over the older polite Ihr and Er. That is why Sie takes the same verb form as "they": Sie kommen.

Ukrainian has more pronoun freedom

Ukrainian can often drop the subject pronoun (Іду додому). German almost never can: Ich gehe nach Hause. The verb ending alone is not enough in German, so the pronoun stays.
