An adjective in German can stand after the verb with no ending at all (Das Auto ist neu) or in front of a noun, where it takes an ending that depends on the article, gender and case (ein neues Auto). The endings are the part everybody fears, but they follow a small system. Once you have it, the other adjective topics (comparison, nouns made from adjectives, participles) reuse the same endings.

Which one when

  • After sein, werden, bleiben: no ending. Der Kaffee ist heiß. That is also how you use most adverbs: Er fährt schnell.
  • In front of a noun: always an ending. Start with Endings and the article tables in Articles.
  • Comparing things: größer als, so groß wie, am größten — see Comparison.
  • No noun after it: the adjective becomes the noun and is capitalised, but keeps its adjective ending: ein Bekannter, die Kranken.
  • A verb used to describe something: gekocht, geöffnet, schlafend behave just like normal adjectives.

The "show the gender once" principle

German wants the gender and case to be visible once before the noun. If the article already shows it (der, dem, des), the adjective relaxes to -e or -en. If the article shows nothing (ein, or no article at all), the adjective takes over the job and gets the "loud" ending: der alte Mann but ein alter Mann. This one idea explains most of the tables.

Ukrainian vs German

Ukrainian adjectives always agree with the noun (новий дім, нова книга, нове авто), even after бути: Дім новий. German drops the ending after the verb: Das Haus ist neu. In front of a noun, German agrees too, but the ending also depends on the article, which Ukrainian doesn't have.

English had this too

Old English had strong and weak adjective endings almost exactly like modern German: gōd mann (a good man) but se gōda mann (the good man). English lost them during the Middle English period. German kept the system, and the terms "strong" and "weak" adjective come from Jacob Grimm.
