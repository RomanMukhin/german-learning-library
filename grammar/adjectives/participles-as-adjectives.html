Participles as adjectives

die geschlossene Tür and das lachende Kind: using Partizip I and II as adjectives, and what each one means.

German verbs have two participles, and both can work as adjectives: Partizip II (geschlossen, closed) and Partizip I (lachend, laughing). In front of a noun they take normal adjective endings: die geschlossene Tür, das lachende Kind. You already use many of them without noticing: gebraucht, bekannt, verheiratet, geöffnet, reserviert... they appear on every shop door and in every form.

A2 core

Partizip II as an adjective

You know the Partizip II from the Perfekt (ich habe gekocht). Put it in front of a noun, add an ending, and it becomes an adjective with a finished, passive meaning:

Verb Partizip II As an adjective
schließen geschlossen die geschlossene Tür — the closed door
kochen gekocht ein gekochtes Ei — a boiled egg
öffnen geöffnet das geöffnete Fenster — the opened window
brauchen gebraucht ein gebrauchtes Auto — a used car
stehlen gestohlen das gestohlene Fahrrad — the stolen bike

After sein there is no ending, just like any adjective: Der Laden ist geschlossen. Das Fenster ist geöffnet.

  • Ich habe ein gebrauchtes Handy gekauft. — I bought a used phone.
  • Das Museum ist montags geschlossen. — The museum is closed on Mondays.
  • Wir essen gekochte Kartoffeln. — We're eating boiled potatoes.

Everyday adjectives that are really participles

Many words you learnt as adjectives are participles: bekannt (known, related to kennen), verheiratet (married), geschieden (divorced), verletzt (injured), verboten (forbidden), beliebt (popular), interessiert, begeistert, verliebt, gestresst.

  • Ich bin verheiratet. — I'm married. (Я одружений.)
  • Rauchen ist hier verboten.
  • Er ist ein bekannter Schauspieler. — He's a well-known actor.

Partizip II = "has been done"

If you can translate it with English "-ed / -en" (closed, broken, stolen), it's Partizip II: the action is finished and was done to the noun. die reparierte Uhr = the watch that has been repaired.

B1

Partizip I: infinitive + d

Partizip I is formed from the infinitive + -d: lachen → lachend, schlafen → schlafend, kommen → kommend. It means the action is happening right now, done by the noun, like English "-ing".

Verb Partizip I Example
lachen lachend das lachende Kind — the laughing child
schlafen schlafend der schlafende Hund — the sleeping dog
kommen kommend in den kommenden Tagen — in the coming days
folgen folgend die folgenden Fragen — the following questions
fließen fließend fließendes Wasser — running water

As an adverb (no ending) it describes how someone does something: Er spricht fließend Deutsch. Sie kam lachend ins Zimmer.

Partizip I vs Partizip II

Partizip I (-end) Partizip II (ge-...-t/-en)
time happening now finished
direction active: the noun does it passive: done to the noun
example die kochende Suppe — the boiling soup die gekochte Suppe — the cooked soup
example der streikende Arbeiter — the striking worker das gestohlene Auto — the stolen car
example ein spannender Film — an exciting film ein gespannter Zuschauer — an excited viewer

-end = "-ing", ge- = "-ed"

The match with English is almost perfect: spannend = exciting, gespannt = excited; langweilend is rare, but langweilig = boring, gelangweilt = bored. If the noun causes the feeling, use -end; if it feels it, use Partizip II.

Classic mistakes

  • das ~~geschlossen~~ Fenster — in front of a noun you need an ending: das geschlossene Fenster.
  • Die Tür ist ~~geschlossene~~ — after sein, no ending.
  • Ich bin ~~langweilig~~ means "I am boring". "I am bored" = Mir ist langweilig or Ich bin gelangweilt.
  • ein ~~gekochende~~ Ei — don't mix forms: ein gekochtes Ei (boiled) or kochendes Wasser (boiling).
  • Partizip I is not a tense: never ~~ich bin lesend~~ for "I am reading". Say ich lese (gerade).

Ukrainian vs German

Ukrainian has дієприкметники too: зачинені двері = die geschlossene Tür, зварене яйце = ein gekochtes Ei. Active present participles in -учий/-ючий (палаючий) are often avoided in Ukrainian, which prefers a clause (дитина, що сміється). German uses Partizip I freely: das lachende Kind.

German loves its participle signs

Look at any German shop or office: Geöffnet (open), Geschlossen (closed), Reserviert (reserved), Besetzt (occupied), Ausverkauft (sold out), Verboten (forbidden). Many German signs are Partizip II forms, because they describe a state that results from an action. Even Achtung, frisch gestrichen! (wet paint, literally "freshly painted") is one.

Later: extended participle phrases

Formal German puts whole clauses in front of a noun: die von der Regierung geplante Reform (the reform planned by the government), der seit Stunden auf den Zug wartende Mann (the man who has been waiting for the train for hours). Read them from the article to the noun, then go back to the middle. In speech, use a relative clause instead: die Reform, die die Regierung geplant hat. See Formal German.
