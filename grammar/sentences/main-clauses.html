Main clauses

Statements in detail: what can stand in position 1, the order of objects and pronouns, and old before new.

A main clause (Hauptsatz) is a clause that can stand alone as a full sentence. A normal statement (Aussagesatz) has the conjugated verb in position 2. That much is covered in Word order; this page looks closer at the two places where German gives you choices: what goes in position 1, and in which order the objects follow.

A2 core

Why German can move things around

In English, the order of the words tells you who does what: "The cat chases the bird" and "The bird chases the cat" are different stories. In German, the case carries this information, so the order is free to do another job: showing what the sentence is about.

  • Die Katze jagt den Vogel. – The cat chases the bird.
  • Den Vogel jagt die Katze. – Same meaning: den shows that the bird is the object. The sentence is now "about" the bird.
  • Der Vogel jagt die Katze. – Now the bird is the subject: a very brave bird.

With feminine, neuter and plural nouns, nominative and accusative look the same (die Frau, das Kind). Then the first noun is normally read as the subject: Die Mutter ruft das Kind – the mother calls the child.

Position 1: one block, any block

The same sentence can start in five different ways. The verb stays in position 2, the participle stays at the end, and the subject moves behind the verb as soon as something else takes position 1:

Position 1 Verb Middle End
Meine Tante hat ihrem Sohn am Samstag in Wien das Fahrrad geschenkt.
Ihrem Sohn hat meine Tante am Samstag in Wien das Fahrrad geschenkt.
Am Samstag hat meine Tante ihrem Sohn in Wien das Fahrrad geschenkt.
In Wien hat meine Tante ihrem Sohn am Samstag das Fahrrad geschenkt.
Das Fahrrad hat meine Tante ihrem Sohn am Samstag in Wien geschenkt.

"My aunt gave her son the bike in Vienna on Saturday." Starting with the subject is the neutral choice. Starting with a time (Am Samstag) is just as normal; starting with an object usually sounds like a contrast: Das Fahrrad hat sie ihm geschenkt, den Helm nicht. – The bike she gave him, not the helmet.

Position 1 can also hold a whole clause or an infinitive group: Wenn es regnet, bleiben wir zu Hause. Um pünktlich zu sein, nehme ich das Taxi.

Start where the last sentence stopped

In stories and texts, put the block that links to what came before into position 1: Wir waren im Zoo. Dort haben wir Pinguine gesehen. Danach sind wir essen gegangen. – We were at the zoo. There we saw penguins. After that we went out to eat. It sounds natural and avoids starting every sentence with Wir.

What does not count as position 1

  • The conjunctions und, aber, oder, denn, sondern: Aber ich habe keine Zeit. (see Conjunctions)
  • ja, nein, doch and interjections, followed by a comma: Ja, ich komme. Ach, das ist schade.
  • A name or phrase you are addressing: Anna, kommst du?

Order of the objects

Many verbs take two objects: a person in the dative and a thing in the accusative (geben, schenken, zeigen, schicken, erklären). Their order depends on whether they are nouns or pronouns:

Objects Order Example
two nouns dative – accusative Ich zeige dem Gast das Zimmer.
pronoun + noun pronoun first Ich zeige ihm das Zimmer. Ich zeige es dem Gast.
two pronouns accusative – dative Ich zeige es ihm.

"I'm showing the guest the room / him the room / it to the guest / it to him."

Short words first

Pronouns are short and point back to things already mentioned, so they come early. The longest, newest piece of information comes last. If you are unsure, ask yourself which block is new for the listener and put it at the end.

B1

Pronouns right after the verb

Unstressed pronouns, including the reflexive sich, jump to the front of the middle field, directly after the verb. They can even come before a noun subject:

  • Gestern hat mir mein Chef eine E-Mail geschrieben. – My boss wrote me an email yesterday.
  • Heute hat sich meine Schwester ein Auto gekauft. – My sister bought herself a car today.
  • Zum Glück hat uns niemand gesehen. – Luckily nobody saw us.

A pronoun subject always comes first among the pronouns: Gestern hat er es mir gegeben. – subject, accusative, dative.

Old before new

Information the listener already knows tends to come early in the middle field; new information goes late, close to the end. This is why the article matters:

  • Ich habe den Schlüssel gestern im Büro gefunden. – the key we were talking about: known, so early.
  • Ich habe gestern im Büro einen Schlüssel gefunden. – some key: new, so after time and place.

In the same way, the block you want to stress can move later: Sie hat das Buch ihrem Bruder geschenkt (to her brother, not to anyone else).

Complements stay close to the end

Some verbs need a place, direction or other complement to be complete (wohnen in, legen auf, fahren nach, sein + adjective). That complement stands last in the middle field, right before the right bracket, even after a time or "how" phrase:

  • Ich habe das Glas vorsichtig auf den Tisch gestellt. – I carefully put the glass on the table.
  • Wir wohnen seit zwei Jahren in Leipzig. – We've lived in Leipzig for two years.
  • Das Essen war gestern wirklich gut. – The food really was good yesterday.

Two main clauses together

With und or oder, a shared subject can be left out in the second clause, but only if that clause also starts with the verb:

  • Ich stehe um sieben auf und (ich) dusche. – I get up at seven and shower.
  • Ich stehe um sieben auf, und dann dusche ich. – dann takes position 1, so the subject must come back after the verb.

Classic mistakes

  • Am Samstag meine Tante hat ... → Am Samstag hat meine Tante ... (only one block before the verb).
  • Ich gebe ihm es → Ich gebe es ihm. (two pronouns: accusative first)
  • Ich zeige das Zimmer ihm → Ich zeige ihm das Zimmer. (pronoun before noun)
  • Es habe ich gestern gekauft → Das habe ich gestern gekauft. Unstressed es as an object cannot start a sentence; use das.

Verb second in other languages

Dutch, Swedish, Norwegian, Danish and Icelandic all put the verb second, just like German. Old English did too; modern English only keeps traces of it in sentences like "Here comes the bus" or "Never have I seen such a mess".
