Participle clauses

Written-style shortcuts like Im Hotel angekommen, ... and Laut lachend, ...: Partizip I vs Partizip II.

A participle clause (Partizipialsatz, also Partizipialgruppe) packs a whole subordinate clause into a few words around a participle: Im Hotel angekommen, rief sie sofort ihre Mutter an. – Having arrived at the hotel, she called her mother straight away. They belong to written, slightly elevated German: novels, reports, newspapers. You need to understand them at B2, and a handful of fixed ones are common even in everyday speech.

B2

Two participles, two meanings

Participle Form Meaning in the clause Example
Partizip I infinitive + -d: lachend, singend, wartend happens at the same time as the main action, active Laut singend, fuhr er durch die Stadt. – Singing loudly, he drove through town.
Partizip II gekommen, gefragt, angekommen happened before the main action, or is passive Zu Hause angekommen, legte sie sich hin. – Having got home, she lay down.

The participle clause has no subject. It always refers to the subject of the main clause: in the examples, er sings and sie arrives.

Word order

  • The participle stands last in its group; everything that belongs to it comes before: Den Brief in der Hand haltend, ... – holding the letter in her hand, ...
  • When the participle clause comes first, it fills position 1, so the main clause continues with its verb: Vom Lärm geweckt, stand er auf. – Woken by the noise, he got up.
  • It can also follow the main clause or sit after the subject: Sie verließ den Raum, leise lachend. Der Zug, aus München kommend, hat zehn Minuten Verspätung.
  • A participle group is usually set off by a comma. A single participle without other words normally takes none: Lachend kam sie herein.

From a full clause to a participle clause

To build one, drop the conjunction, the subject and the auxiliary verb, and keep the participle at the end:

Full clause Participle clause
Während sie leise sang, kochte sie das Essen. Leise singend, kochte sie das Essen.
Nachdem er in Rom angekommen war, suchte er ein Hotel. In Rom angekommen, suchte er ein Hotel.
Weil sie vom Lärm geweckt worden war, stand sie auf. Vom Lärm geweckt, stand sie auf.
Wenn man es genau nimmt, stimmt das nicht. Genau genommen stimmt das nicht.

Going the other way is the useful skill for reading: when you meet a participle clause, ask "while ...?", "after ...?" or "because ...?" and rebuild the full clause in your head.

Fixed expressions you will hear every day

A few participle clauses have become set phrases. They usually stand in position 1 and are often followed directly by the verb (no comma needed in most of them):

  • Ehrlich gesagt habe ich keine Lust. – To be honest, I don't feel like it.
  • Kurz gesagt: Es war ein Erfolg. – In short: it was a success.
  • Genau genommen ist das kein Fehler. – Strictly speaking, that isn't a mistake.
  • Im Grunde genommen hast du recht. – Basically, you're right.
  • Abgesehen davon ist alles in Ordnung. – Apart from that, everything's fine.
  • Verglichen mit letztem Jahr ist es billiger. – Compared to last year, it's cheaper.
  • Angenommen, du gewinnst im Lotto: Was machst du? – Suppose you win the lottery: what would you do?

Spoken German: use a full clause

Outside the fixed phrases, participle clauses sound bookish in conversation. When you speak, say Als ich angekommen bin, ... or Nachdem ich angekommen war, ... instead of Angekommen, .... Save the short form for written texts.

The dangling participle

The participle clause must fit the subject of the main clause. Im Hotel angekommen, klingelte das Telefon literally says that the telephone arrived at the hotel. Correct: Im Hotel angekommen, hörte ich das Telefon klingeln, or use a full clause: Als ich im Hotel ankam, klingelte das Telefon. (The fixed phrases like ehrlich gesagt are exceptions.)

Participle clause or participle adjective?

Don't confuse a participle clause with a participle used as an adjective before a noun. The adjective version takes an ending and has no comma: die schlafenden Kinder (the sleeping children), der gestern angekommene Gast (the guest who arrived yesterday). See Participles as adjectives.

A Latin habit

German writers borrowed much of this style from Latin, whose participle constructions were imitated by scholars and translators for centuries. Modern style guides usually advise using them sparingly, because a long participle group delays the main clause.
