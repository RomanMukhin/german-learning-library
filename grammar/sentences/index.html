German sentence building follows a few strict rules, and almost all of them are about where the verb goes. Once you know the three positions of the verb (first, second, last), questions, negation and long sentences with weil or dass become much easier.

Overview

Topic Key rule Example Page
Questions W-question: verb 2nd; yes/no: verb 1st Wann kommst du? / Kommst du? Questions
Negation kein for nouns without a definite article, nicht for the rest Ich habe kein Auto. Ich komme nicht. Negation
Word order verb 2nd in main clauses, time before manner before place Morgen fahre ich mit dem Zug nach Berlin. Word order
Subordinate clauses conjugated verb at the end ..., weil ich müde bin. Subordinate clauses

The three verb positions

Verb position Where Example
1st yes/no questions, imperatives Hast du Zeit? Komm her!
2nd statements, W-questions Ich habe Zeit. Wann hast du Zeit?
Last subordinate clauses (dass, weil, ob, wenn...) ..., dass du Zeit hast.

Which one when

  • Want to ask something? Start with Questions.
  • Unsure between nicht and kein? See Negation.
  • Your sentence has time, place and "how" in it? Check Word order.
  • Joining two ideas with because, that, when, although? Go to Subordinate clauses.

Count to two

In any normal statement, put exactly one block before the verb: Ich / Heute / Im Sommer / Meinem Bruder + verb. Anything you move to the front pushes the subject behind the verb: Heute gehe ich ins Kino. If you feel two blocks before the verb, the sentence is wrong.

Mark Twain and the German verb

Mark Twain struggled with German and mocked it in his essay The Awful German Language (1880). In A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court (1889) he wrote that when a literary German dives into a sentence, you will not see him again "till he emerges on the other side of his Atlantic with his verb in his mouth". He was describing the verb-at-the-end rule.
