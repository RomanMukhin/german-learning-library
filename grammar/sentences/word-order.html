Word order

The map of a German sentence: three verb positions, the verb bracket, and the order of everything in between.

German word order looks free, but the verb is fixed. This page gives you the map of a German sentence: where the verb can stand, how its two parts frame the sentence, and the default order of everything in between. The details of statements are in Main clauses; the full reference is in Word order (reference).

A2 core

Three places for the verb

Every German clause puts its conjugated verb in one of three places. The type of clause decides which one:

Verb position Clause type Example
1st yes/no questions, imperatives, conditions without wenn Kommst du mit? – Are you coming? Komm mit! – Come along!
2nd statements, W-questions Morgen kommt sie. – She's coming tomorrow. Wann kommt sie? – When is she coming?
Last subordinate clauses: dass, weil, ob, wenn, relative clauses, indirect questions ..., weil sie morgen kommt. – ... because she's coming tomorrow.

Rule 1: the verb is second

In a statement the conjugated verb is always in position 2. Position 1 can be the subject or anything else, but only one block. A block can be long (Am letzten Samstag im Mai), but it is still one block:

Position 1 Verb Rest
Ich fahre morgen nach Berlin.
Morgen fahre ich nach Berlin.
Nach Berlin fahre ich morgen.
Am letzten Samstag im Mai fahre ich nach Berlin.

All four mean "I'm going to Berlin tomorrow / on the last Saturday in May". What changes is the starting point, not the meaning.

The verb bracket

If the verb has two parts (haben/sein + participle, modal + infinitive, werden + infinitive, separable verbs), the conjugated part stays in position 2 and the second part goes to the end. Together they form a bracket (Satzklammer) around the rest of the sentence:

  • Ich habe gestern einen Film gesehen. – I saw a film yesterday.
  • Wir müssen heute früh aufstehen. – We have to get up early today.
  • Der Zug kommt um acht an. – The train arrives at eight.
  • Es wird morgen regnen. – It will rain tomorrow.

With three verbs, the last two sit together at the end: Ich habe das nicht machen wollen. – I didn't want to do that (B2).

Swap, don't add

When you put heute, dann, leider or anything else in front, the subject simply swaps with the verb: Ich gehe → Heute gehe ich. Never Heute ich gehe, a classic mistake for English speakers.

und, aber, oder do not count

The conjunctions und, aber, oder, denn, sondern stand in "position 0": they join two main clauses without moving the verb. Ich bin müde, denn ich habe schlecht geschlafen. Compare weil, which sends the verb to the end, and deshalb, which takes position 1 itself. All three kinds are compared in Conjunctions and clause order.

B1

The sentence map

Grammars describe the German sentence as five "fields". Once you see them, every clause type follows the same plan; only the left bracket changes:

Front field Left bracket Middle field Right bracket End field
Gestern hat Anna ihrem Bruder das Buch gegeben.
Hat Anna ihrem Bruder das Buch gegeben?
Wem hat Anna das Buch gegeben?
dass Anna ihrem Bruder das Buch gegeben hat
Ich habe mehr gegessen als du.
  • Front field (position 1): exactly one block in statements and W-questions; empty in yes/no questions and subordinate clauses.
  • Left bracket: the conjugated verb, or in a subordinate clause the conjunction (dass, weil...).
  • Middle field: subject (if not in front), objects, adverbs, nicht. This is where most order questions arise.
  • Right bracket: participle, infinitive, separable prefix; in subordinate clauses also the conjugated verb.
  • End field: things that may follow the bracket, mainly comparisons with als/wie and whole clauses: Ich habe vergessen, dass heute Montag ist.

Rule 2: order inside the middle field

The middle field has a default order. You only need four rules:

  1. Pronouns first, right after the verb (or the conjunction), even before a noun subject: Gestern hat ihn mein Bruder besucht. – My brother visited him yesterday.
  2. Two nouns: dative before accusative: Ich gebe dem Kind den Ball. Two pronouns: accusative before dative: Ich gebe ihn ihm. Details in Main clauses.
  3. Adverbials: Te-Ka-Mo-Lo, see below.
  4. nicht goes late, before the right bracket and before place and "how" phrases: Ich habe ihn gestern nicht gesehen. See Negation.

TeKaMoLo

When several adverbials come together, the default order is Temporal (when) – Kausal (why) – Modal (how) – Lokal (where):

  • Ich fahre morgen (Te) wegen der Arbeit (Ka) mit dem Zug (Mo) nach Hamburg (Lo). – I'm going to Hamburg by train tomorrow for work.
  • Sie ist gestern mit dem Fahrrad zur Uni gefahren. – She cycled to university yesterday.

TeKaMoLo is a default, not a law: any of these blocks can move to position 1 (Mit dem Zug fahre ich morgen nach Hamburg), and a place that completes the verb (wohnen in, fahren nach, stellen auf) likes to stand last, right before the bracket.

Time before place

If you only remember one thing from TeKaMoLo, remember "when" before "where": Ich gehe heute ins Kino, not Ich gehe ins Kino heute. English does the opposite ("to the cinema today").

Rule 3: verb at the end in subordinate clauses

After dass, weil, wenn, ob, obwohl, relative pronouns and question words in indirect questions, the conjugated verb goes to the very end:

  • Ich glaube, dass er morgen kommt. – I think he's coming tomorrow.
  • Ich bleibe zu Hause, weil ich krank bin. – I'm staying home because I'm ill.
  • Ich weiß nicht, ob sie mitkommen will. – I don't know if she wants to come along.
  • ..., dass der Film um acht anfängt. – separable verbs join up again.

If the subordinate clause comes first, it fills position 1, so the main clause starts with the verb: Weil ich krank bin, bleibe ich zu Hause. More in Subordinate clauses.

Classic mistakes

  • Heute ich habe keine Zeit → Heute habe ich keine Zeit.
  • Ich habe gesehen den Film → Ich habe den Film gesehen.
  • ..., weil ich bin müde → ..., weil ich müde bin.
  • Wenn ich Zeit habe, ich komme → Wenn ich Zeit habe, komme ich.
  • Gestern mein Bruder hat ihn besucht → Gestern hat ihn mein Bruder besucht.

Later: verb clusters in subordinate clauses

With a modal in the Perfekt, the conjugated haben jumps in front of the two infinitives instead of going last: ..., weil ich nicht habe kommen können. It is the one exception to "conjugated verb last".
