German verbs are where most sentence mistakes come from. The endings are the easy part. The harder part is what the verb demands from the rest of the sentence: a helper verb, a case, a preposition, a reflexive pronoun, an infinitive at the end or a prefix that moves away. This section groups verbs by that behaviour.

Basic terms

Weak, strong and mixed

Type What happens Example
weak (regular) stem never changes; Präteritum -te, Partizip ge- ... -t spielen – spielte – gespielt
strong (irregular) vowel change in the Präteritum and often the Partizip; Partizip ge- ... -en finden – fand – gefunden
mixed vowel change like a strong verb, but endings -te and -t like a weak verb bringen – brachte – gebracht

You cannot see from the infinitive which type a verb is, so learn the three forms of every new verb. The most frequent ones are on the 50 most common verbs.

Main verbs, helpers and modals

A main verb carries the meaning (Wir bauen ein Haus). The helper verbs haben, sein, werden build tenses and the passive (Wir haben ein Haus gebaut). Modal verbs add ability, permission or obligation (Wir wollen ein Haus bauen).

Finite and non-finite forms

The finite verb is the conjugated one; it agrees with the subject and stands in position 2 of a main clause: Morgen fahre ich nach Bern. The non-finite forms never change and go to the end: the infinitive (Ich will nach Bern fahren), the infinitive with zu (Ich plane, nach Bern zu fahren) and the participles (Ich bin nach Bern gefahren).

Personal and impersonal verbs

Most verbs work with every person: ich gehe, du gehst, ... A few only make sense with es. Weather verbs are the classic case: es regnet, es schneit, es donnert, es blitzt. Some fixed expressions work the same way: es gibt (there is/are), es geht um (it is about), es klingelt (someone is at the door). Others are normally used only in the third person: Was ist passiert? Das geschieht oft.

Verbs also have moods: the normal indicative, the imperative for commands and the Konjunktiv II for wishes and polite or unreal statements.

Which page when

  • You are just starting and want the verbs you will use most: the 50 most common verbs.
  • You need to say I am / I have / it is getting: start with sein, haben, werden.
  • Ich habe gegangen or ich bin gegangen? haben or sein in the Perfekt.
  • You want to say can, must, may, want: modal verbs.
  • The English verb has no object but German puts sich in: reflexive verbs.
  • You know the verb but not the little word after it (warten auf? warten für?): verbs with prepositions.
  • Ich helfe dich sounds right to you: dative verbs.
  • Two verbs in one sentence and you are not sure about zu: zu + infinitive.
  • The prefix of your verb is suddenly at the end of the sentence: separable verbs.
  • legen or liegen, setzen or sitzen? Transitive and intransitive verbs.
  • You are not sure how to build gemacht, studiert, angerufen or what die wartenden Gäste means: participles.
  • Something "is done" and nobody says by whom: the passive.

Learn the verb with its "luggage"

Never learn a bare verb. Learn the whole pattern: sich freuen auf + Akk, helfen + Dat, anfangen, fängt an, hat angefangen. One flashcard per pattern saves you three mistakes later.

Almost every German verb ends in -en

The infinitive ending -en (or -n in tun, sein, ändern) is a leftover of an old Germanic ending that English lost completely: Old English still had singan for "to sing". That is why English needs the little word to and German does not.
