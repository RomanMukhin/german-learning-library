German verbs are where most sentence mistakes come from. The endings are the easy part. The harder part is what the verb demands from the rest of the sentence: a helper verb, a case, a preposition, a reflexive pronoun, an infinitive at the end or a prefix that moves away. This section groups verbs by that behaviour.

Verb types at a glance

Type What is special Example Page
sein, haben, werden irregular, also used as helper verbs Ich habe Hunger. Es wird kalt. sein, haben, werden
Modal verbs no ending in ich/er, second verb goes to the end as an infinitive Ich kann heute nicht kommen. Modal verbs
Reflexive verbs need mich/mir, dich/dir, sich Ich freue mich. Reflexive verbs
Verbs + preposition fixed preposition with a fixed case Ich warte auf den Bus. Verbs with prepositions
Dative verbs the object is dative, not accusative Ich helfe dir. Dative verbs
zu + infinitive a second verb with zu at the end Es ist schwer, Deutsch zu lernen. zu + infinitive
Separable verbs prefix jumps to the end of the clause Ich rufe dich morgen an. Separable verbs

The forms of strong and irregular verbs (gehen – ging – gegangen) are collected in the irregular verbs table.

Which page when

  • You need to say I am / I have / it is getting: start with sein, haben, werden.
  • You want to say can, must, may, want: modal verbs.
  • The English verb has no object but German puts sich in: reflexive verbs.
  • You know the verb but not the little word after it (warten auf? warten für?): verbs with prepositions.
  • Ich helfe dich sounds right to you: dative verbs.
  • Two verbs in one sentence and you are not sure about zu: zu + infinitive.
  • The prefix of your verb is suddenly at the end of the sentence: separable verbs.

Learn the verb with its "luggage"

Never learn a bare verb. Learn the whole pattern: sich freuen auf + Akk, helfen + Dat, anfangen, fängt an, hat angefangen. One flashcard per pattern saves you three mistakes later.

Almost every German verb ends in -en

The infinitive ending -en (or -n in tun, sein, ändern) is a leftover of an old Germanic ending that English lost completely: Old English still had singan for "to sing". That is why English needs the little word to and German does not.
