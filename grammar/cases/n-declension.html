N-declension

Weak masculine nouns like der Junge, der Student and der Herr that add -n in every case except the nominative.

Most German nouns hardly change in the singular; the article does the work. A group of masculine nouns is different: they add -n or -en in every case except the nominative singular. This is the n-Deklination (weak masculine nouns). It matters because some of the most common words belong here: der Herr, der Kollege, der Student, der Mensch, der Nachbar.

A2 core

The pattern

der Junge der Student der Mensch
Nom der Junge der Student der Mensch
Akk den Jungen den Studenten den Menschen
Dat dem Jungen dem Studenten dem Menschen
Gen des Jungen des Studenten des Menschen
Plural die Jungen die Studenten die Menschen

Only the nominative singular has no ending. Everything else ends in -(e)n, and the plural looks the same.

  • Ich kenne den Jungen. — I know the boy.
  • Ich helfe dem Studenten. — I'm helping the student.
  • Das ist das Buch des Kollegen. — That is the colleague's book.
  • Hast du unseren Nachbarn gesehen? — Have you seen our neighbour?
  • Sehr geehrter Herr Müller — but: Ich habe Herrn Müller angerufen.
  • Jeder Mensch braucht Freunde. — Wir helfen jedem Menschen.

Der Herr: -n singular, -en plural

der Herr is slightly special: singular Herrn (den/dem/des Herrn), plural Herren (Meine Damen und Herren!). In letters: Sehr geehrter Herr Schmidt (nominative), but on the envelope or in a sentence: an Herrn Schmidt, mit Herrn Schmidt.

The "-e + der + alive" test

If a noun is masculine, ends in -e and is a person or animal, it is almost certainly weak: der Junge, der Kollege, der Kunde, der Löwe, der Affe, der Hase, der Russe, der Franzose. This one rule covers most weak nouns you will meet.

B1

How to recognise weak nouns

Group Examples
masculine, -e, people and animals der Junge, der Kollege, der Kunde, der Neffe, der Löwe, der Affe
nationalities in -e der Pole, der Russe, der Türke, der Franzose, der Schwede
foreign words in -ent, -ant, -ist, -at, -oge, -nom der Student, der Präsident, der Elefant, der Polizist, der Tourist, der Soldat, der Biologe, der Astronom
a few short old words der Mensch, der Herr, der Bär, der Nachbar, der Bauer, der Held, der Prinz, der Fotograf

der Deutsche looks similar but is an adjective used as a noun (ein Deutscher): see Adjectives as nouns.

Note: nationalities in -er are normal nouns: der Ukrainer → den Ukrainer, der Engländer → den Engländer. Only those in -e are weak.

The dictionary tells you

A dictionary entry like Student, der; -en, -en means genitive -en and plural -en: weak. A normal noun looks like Tisch, der; -(e)s, -e: genitive with -s. If the genitive ending is -n/-en, add it in the accusative and dative too. More on genders and plurals: Noun gender and plurals.

The mixed group: der Name

A handful of nouns take -n like weak nouns but -ns in the genitive: der Name, der Buchstabe, der Gedanke, der Glaube, der Wille, der Friede.

Nom Akk Dat Gen
der Name den Namen dem Namen des Namens
der Gedanke den Gedanken dem Gedanken des Gedankens
  • Wie schreibt man deinen Namen? — How do you spell your name?
  • Ich habe keinen Gedanken daran verschwendet.

das Herz is the one neuter odd one: das Herz, dem Herzen, des Herzens (accusative stays das Herz).

Classic mistakes

  • Ich frage ~~den Student~~ — weak: den Studenten.
  • Ich schreibe ~~Herr~~ Meier eine E-Mail — dative: Herrn Meier.
  • Wie ist ~~dein Namen~~? — nominative has no -n: dein Name; but Ich kenne deinen Namen.
  • Don't over-apply it: der Lehrer, der Arzt, der Ukrainer are normal: den Lehrer, dem Arzt.
  • In spoken German the -n is often dropped (mit dem Student), but in writing and exams it counts as a mistake.

Ukrainian vs German

In Ukrainian every noun changes its ending (студент, студента, студентові), so the idea is familiar. The German weak nouns are the one place where the noun itself, not only the article, carries the case in the singular: den Studenten ~ студента. Think of the German -n as a small Ukrainian ending.

Why they are called "weak"

The terms "strong" and "weak" declension come from Jacob Grimm (of the Brothers Grimm), who used them in his Deutsche Grammatik (1819 onwards) for verbs and nouns. Weak nouns were once a much bigger class, and many have since moved to the normal pattern. Compounds sometimes still show the old -n: der Hahn is a normal noun today, but the rooster's comb is still der Hahnenkamm. The group keeps shrinking, which is why spoken German often drops the -n.
