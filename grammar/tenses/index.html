German has six tenses on paper, but in everyday speech you need about three: the present, the Perfekt, and the Präteritum of a handful of verbs. This page puts them all on one timeline and tells you which to use where.

The timeline

Tense Form Example Time Speech Writing
Plusquamperfekt hatte/war + Partizip II Ich hatte schon gegessen. before another past event sometimes yes
Perfekt haben/sein + Partizip II Ich habe gegessen. past main past tense letters, chats
Präteritum one word: ich aß Ich aß. past only war, hatte, modals main past tense in stories, news
Präsens ich esse Ich esse. now, habits, future with a time word yes yes
Futur I werden + infinitive Ich werde essen. future, plans, guesses for emphasis or guesses yes
Futur II werden + Partizip II + haben/sein Ich werde gegessen haben. finished by a future point rare rare (B2)

Which one when

  • Talking about now, habits and most of the future: present. Morgen fahre ich nach Berlin.
  • Telling a friend what happened: Perfekt. Ich habe gestern lange gearbeitet.
  • With sein, haben and modal verbs, even in speech: Präteritum. Ich war müde, ich musste schlafen.
  • Writing a story, report or news text: Präteritum for everything.
  • Promises, predictions, guesses: Futur I. Das wird schon klappen.

Three tenses cover 95% of speech

If you master ich mache, ich habe gemacht and the five Präteritum forms war, hatte, konnte, musste, wollte, you can talk about almost anything. Everything else is for reading and writing.

The tense that never left the south

In Bavaria, Austria and Switzerland the spoken Präteritum disappeared centuries ago: people often say ich bin gewesen where northerners say ich war. Linguists call this loss Präteritumschwund. Swiss German dialects have no simple past at all, apart from a few relics.
