Formal and informal address

du, ihr and Sie, who uses which, how to offer du, and how to open and close emails and letters.

German has three words for "you": du (one person, informal), ihr (several people, informal) and Sie (one or more people, formal). Choosing between duzen (saying du) and siezen (saying Sie) is a social decision, not just grammar, and the rules differ between Germany, Austria, Switzerland and even between offices. You need this from your first sentence to a stranger.

A2 core

The forms

du (informal, 1 person) ihr (informal, plural) Sie (formal, 1 or more)
Nominativ du ihr Sie
Akkusativ dich euch Sie
Dativ dir euch Ihnen
Possessive dein euer Ihr
Verb du kommst ihr kommt Sie kommen
Imperative Komm! Kommt! Kommen Sie!

The formal forms are always capitalised: Sie, Ihnen, Ihr. Lowercase sie, ihnen, ihr means "she / they, them, her / their". Können Sie mir helfen? = Can you help me?; Können sie mir helfen? = Can they help me?

The verb with Sie is the same as with sie (they): 3rd person plural, even when you talk to one person.

Who gets which

Situation Usual choice
family, friends, children, animals, God in prayer du
other students, young people at parties, online gaming, sports clubs du
strangers in the street, shop staff, officials, police, doctors Sie
older people you do not know Sie
colleagues in a traditional German company, bank, office Sie (often until someone offers du)
IT start-ups, Swedish and US companies, many young teams du from day one, even with the boss
children up to about 14–16 they get du; teachers start saying Sie in upper secondary school

Examples: - Entschuldigung, können Sie mir helfen? — Excuse me, can you help me? (stranger) - Wie heißen Sie? – Wie heißt du? — What is your name? (formal / informal) - Das ist für Sie, Frau Schmidt. — This is for you, Ms Schmidt. - Ich danke Ihnen, Herr Klein. — Thank you, Mr Klein. - Ist das Ihr Koffer? — Is that your suitcase? - Kinder, habt ihr Hunger? — Kids, are you hungry?

Herr, Frau and titles

With Sie you use Herr / Frau + surname: Guten Tag, Frau Schmidt. Never Frau Anna. Fräulein (for unmarried women) is outdated and can sound patronising: every adult woman is Frau. With du you use the first name.

Academic titles go between: Herr Dr. Müller, Frau Professorin Weber. In Germany the Dr. is used in letters and official contexts; in Austria titles are used much more in speech too (Frau Magister, Herr Doktor, Herr Ingenieur).

When in doubt, Sie

With adults you do not know, always start with Sie. If the other person says du to you first and is your age or younger, you can answer with du. If they are older or your boss, wait until they offer it.

B1

Offering du

Switching to du is a small ritual. The older person, the higher-ranking person (boss, teacher, host), or traditionally the woman offers it:

  • Wollen wir uns duzen? — Shall we use du?
  • Ich bin übrigens Thomas. — By the way, I'm Thomas. (a hint: use my first name and du)
  • Sag doch einfach du zu mir. — Just call me du.

You accept with Gerne! Ich bin Roman. Once you have switched, switching back to Sie is very awkward and signals a broken relationship. That is why Germans offer du carefully at work.

Regional and workplace differences

  • Switzerland: du is more common at work than in Germany, also with bosses in many companies.
  • Austria: more Sie and many titles in offices and with officials, but very quick du in the mountains, ski huts and among young people.
  • Germany: big differences. Banks, law firms, public offices: Sie. Start-ups, IT, advertising, NGOs: du. Universities: students say du to each other, Sie to professors.
  • Shops: Sie, but in trendy cafés and shops for young people (and at IKEA) the staff say du.

Emails and letters

Formal (Sie) Neutral / semi-formal Informal (du)
Opening Sehr geehrte Frau Weber, / Sehr geehrter Herr Dr. Müller, / Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren, Guten Tag Frau Weber, / Hallo Herr Müller, Liebe Anna, / Lieber Max, / Hallo Anna, / Hi,
Closing Mit freundlichen Grüßen Viele Grüße / Beste Grüße Liebe Grüße (LG) / Bis bald

Note the endings: geehrte Frau, geehrter Herr; Liebe Anna, Lieber Max. After the opening comma the email starts with a small letter: Sehr geehrte Frau Weber, vielen Dank für Ihre E-Mail. In informal letters you may also capitalise Du / Dein as a sign of respect.

The email ladder

Match the closing to the opening: Sehr geehrte → Mit freundlichen Grüßen; Hallo / Guten Tag → Viele Grüße; Liebe → Liebe Grüße. If the other person writes back one step lower, you can follow them one step down.

Classic mistakes

  • Wie ~~heißt~~ Sie? → Wie heißen Sie? Sie always takes the -en verb.
  • Ich danke ~~Sie~~ → Ich danke Ihnen. danken takes the dative.
  • ~~sie~~ / ~~ihnen~~ in an email to a client means "she / them". Capitalise.
  • ~~Frau Anna~~ → Frau Schmidt or just Anna (with du).
  • Saying du to a police officer can be treated as an insult; in Germany it has even led to fines.

From Ihrzen to Sie

German has changed its polite "you" several times. In the early Middle Ages people addressed a single superior with the plural Ihr (Ihrzen), a "plural of respect", probably copied from the Latin habit of addressing the Roman emperor with vos. In the 17th century a new form, Er / Sie (3rd person singular, Erzen), appeared: "Hat Er das gemacht?" Friedrich II of Prussia was known for using it to his subordinates. Then the 3rd person plural Sie spread, and by the 18th century it was the standard polite form, with Er and Ihr pushed down to address servants and lower ranks. Old Ihr lives on in fairy tales and fantasy films (Was wünscht Ihr, Herr?). English went through the same first step and then took a different turn: you was once the plural and polite form (like Ihr) and thou the informal singular (like du), until you pushed thou out completely. German kept both, which is why it still has a choice to make.

The 20th century pushed the other way. The 1968 student movement used du everywhere as an anti-authoritarian sign, and it has been normal among students ever since. IKEA brought the Swedish habit to German advertising with its du slogan Wohnst du noch oder lebst du schon? ("Do you still just reside, or do you already live?", used since the early 2000s), and many brands followed.

Later: the Hamburger Sie and Münchner Du

Two regional mixes: the Hamburger Sie is first name + Sie (Anna, können Sie mir helfen?), common among colleagues; the Münchner Du is du + surname (Müller, kannst du mal kommen?), heard in workshops and some workplaces. Both are informal hybrids; do not start them yourself.
