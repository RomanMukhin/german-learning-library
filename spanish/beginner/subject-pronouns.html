Yo

A subject pronoun stands for the person or thing doing the action: I, you, she, we, they. Here is the full Spanish set at a glance:

Singular Plural
1st person yo — I nosotros / nosotras — we
2nd person, informal tú (vos) — you vosotros / vosotras — you all (Spain)
2nd person, formal usted — you ustedes — you all
3rd person él — he, ella — she ellos / ellas — they

Yo means I. Unlike English I, it is written with a small letter unless it starts a sentence.

  • Yo trabajo en un hospital. — I work in a hospital.
  • Mi novia y yo vivimos juntos. — My girlfriend and I live together.

Usually left out

Spanish verb endings already show who the subject is: hablo can only mean "I speak". So native speakers normally drop the pronoun and use it only for emphasis or contrast.

  • Hablo tres idiomas. — I speak three languages. (neutral)
  • Yo pago, tú no. — I'm paying, not you. (contrast)
  • —¿Quién quiere café? —Yo. — Who wants coffee? — Me.

Not "me"

In short answers English says me; Spanish says yo: —¿Quién es? —Soy yo. — Who is it? — It's me.

Tú, Usted, Vos

Spanish has several words for singular you, and choosing the right one is a matter of politeness and region.

Tú — informal

Use tú with friends, family, children, classmates, people your age, and pets. It is written with an accent to tell it apart from tu (your).

  • ¿Tú tienes hermanos? — Do you have siblings?
  • Tu casa es bonita. — Your house is pretty. (tu = your, no accent)

Usted — formal

Use usted with people you don't know, older people, customers, officials and anyone you want to show respect to. It is often abbreviated Ud. or Vd.

Usted takes the same verb form as él / ella (third person), because historically it came from vuestra merced, "your grace".

  • ¿Usted es la señora Pérez? — Are you Mrs Pérez?
  • ¿Cómo se llama usted? — What's your name? (formal)

Vos — informal in some countries

In Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay and much of Central America, vos replaces tú as the everyday informal "you". In Chile, Bolivia and a few other countries it lives alongside tú. It has its own verb forms in the present: vos hablás, vos tenés, vos sos.

  • Vos sos muy amable. — You're very kind. (Argentina)

When in doubt, start with usted

If you're not sure, use usted. The other person will usually say ¿Nos tuteamos? or Puedes tutearme ("you can call me tú") if they'd rather be informal.

Él, Ella

Él means he and ella means she. They refer to people, and in Spanish can also refer to animals and things (it), although for things the pronoun is almost always dropped.

  • Él es mi hermano. — He's my brother.
  • Ella estudia medicina. — She studies medicine.
  • —¿Y el museo? —Está cerca. — And the museum? — It's close by. (no pronoun)

The accent matters

Él carries a written accent to distinguish it from the article el (the):

  • El chico canta. — The boy sings.
  • Él canta. — He sings.

Using them for clarity

Because él, ella and usted all share one verb form (come = he eats / she eats / you eat), the pronoun is often added when the context could be unclear:

  • Ella trabaja por la mañana y él por la tarde. — She works in the morning and he in the afternoon.

No pronoun for "it"

There is no subject pronoun for it in neutral sentences. Llueve. — It's raining. Es importante. — It's important. Never Él es importante for an idea.

Nosotros, Nosotras

Nosotros and nosotras mean we — a group that includes the speaker.

Form Used for
nosotros a group of men, or a mixed group
nosotras a group made up only of women

Even a group of ten women and one man takes nosotros. The masculine is the default form for mixed groups across Spanish grammar.

  • Nosotros vamos al cine esta noche. — We're going to the cinema tonight.
  • Nosotras somos compañeras de piso. — We (women) are flatmates.

Like other pronouns, nosotros is usually dropped since the ending -mos already says "we": Comemos a las dos. — We eat at two.

Mi amigo y yo

When "we" is spelled out, the verb is still in the nosotros form:

  • Mi hermana y yo jugamos al tenis. — My sister and I play tennis.

The -mos ending

In almost every Spanish tense, the nosotros form ends in -mos: hablamos, comemos, vivimos, estamos. Spot -mos, think "we".

Ustedes, Vosotros, Vosotras

For plural you (you all, you guys), the choice depends on where you are.

Region Informal plural Formal plural
Spain vosotros / vosotras ustedes
Latin America ustedes ustedes

Vosotros, vosotras — Spain only

Spaniards use vosotros (men or mixed groups) and vosotras (women only) for friends, family and people they'd address as tú individually. These have their own verb forms: vosotros habláis, coméis, vivís.

  • ¿Vosotros venís a la fiesta? — Are you guys coming to the party? (Spain)

Ustedes — everywhere

In Latin America ustedes covers all plural "you", whether talking to friends or strangers. In Spain it is formal. It is abbreviated Uds. or Vds. and takes the same verb form as ellos / ellas.

  • ¿Ustedes son de aquí? — Are you from here? (plural)
  • Chicos, ¿ustedes quieren pizza? — Guys, do you want pizza? (Latin America)

Which should you learn?

Learn to recognise all forms, but practise the system of the region you'll visit most. A Mexican who hears vosotros understands it perfectly — it just sounds Spanish.

Ellos, Ellas

Ellos and ellas mean they.

Form Used for
ellos a group of men, or a mixed group
ellas a group made up only of women

They follow the same gender rule as nosotros / nosotras. For things, the pronoun is normally left out.

  • Ellos trabajan en un banco. — They work in a bank.
  • Ellas son mis primas. — They're my cousins (all female).
  • —¿Dónde están las llaves? —Están en la mesa. — Where are the keys? — They're on the table.

Ellos, ellas and ustedes share a verb form

Hablan can mean "they speak" or "you all speak". Add the pronoun when you need to be clear:

  • Ellos comen aquí, pero ustedes comen en casa. — They eat here, but you eat at home.

Not for objects as subjects

Ellos son caros about shoes sounds odd. Simply say Son caros. — They're expensive.

Comprehensive Review 1

Match each pronoun with its verb form. The present of ser (to be) is a good model:

Pronoun ser English
yo soy I am
tú eres you are
vos sos you are (Arg., etc.)
él, ella, usted es he/she is, you are
nosotros/as somos we are
vosotros/as sois you all are (Spain)
ellos, ellas, ustedes son they are, you all are

Practice: which pronoun?

  • Talking to your boss → usted
  • Talking to your little brother → tú (or vos)
  • Talking to three friends in Madrid → vosotros
  • Talking to three friends in Bogotá → ustedes
  • Talking about Ana and Lucía → ellas
  • Talking about Ana and Pedro → ellos

Comprehensive Review 2

When to keep, when to drop

Keep the pronoun for contrast, emphasis or clarity. Drop it everywhere else.

  • Trabajo mucho. — I work a lot. (neutral)
  • Yo trabajo mucho, pero él no. — I work a lot, but he doesn't. (contrast)
  • ¿Usted habla inglés? — Do you speak English? (clarity: not "he/she")

Spot the mistake

  • Tu eres mi mejor amigo. → Tú eres mi mejor amigo. — You're my best friend.
  • Él es lunes. → Es lunes. — It's Monday.
  • Nosotras somos Juan y Marta. → Nosotros somos Juan y Marta. — We're Juan and Marta.
  • Usted eres muy amable. → Usted es muy amable. — You're very kind.

Usted thinks like él

Whatever verb form you use for él, use for usted; whatever you use for ellos, use for ustedes. That one rule saves a lot of memorising.
