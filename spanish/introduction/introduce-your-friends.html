Introducing Someone

To present a friend, point them out with este (this, for a man) or esta (for a woman) and the verb ser:

  • Este es mi amigo Javier. — This is my friend Javier.
  • Esta es Paula, mi compañera de trabajo. — This is Paula, my coworker.
  • Estos son mis padres. — These are my parents.

More formal ways to introduce people:

Spanish English
Te presento a Luis. Let me introduce Luis to you. (informal)
Le presento a la señora Vega. May I introduce Mrs Vega? (formal)
Les presento a mi esposo. Let me introduce my husband to you all.
Quiero presentarte a… I'd like you to meet…

The person you introduce comes after a, the "personal a" used before people.

Typical replies:

  • Mucho gusto. — Nice to meet you.
  • Encantado. / Encantada. — Delighted. (said by a man / a woman)
  • Igualmente. — Likewise.
  • El gusto es mío. — The pleasure is mine.

Add one detail

After the name, add who they are or what they do: Esta es Marta. Es mi vecina y es arquitecta. It gives the other person something to talk about.

Job Words: Masculine and Feminine

Spanish job nouns usually change form depending on whether the person is a man or a woman, and the article changes with them:

Man Woman English
el panadero la panadera baker
el profesor la profesora teacher
el periodista la periodista journalist
el actor la actriz actor / actress

There are four main patterns, each covered in the sections below:

  1. -o becomes -a (médico → médica)
  2. a final consonant adds -a (doctor → doctora)
  3. -ista, -e and some -a words don't change (la dentista, el cantante)
  4. a few special feminine forms (alcalde → alcaldesa)

To talk about a job, use ser + job without an article:

  • Javier es ingeniero. — Javier is an engineer.
  • Mis tías son enfermeras. — My aunts are nurses.

To ask: ¿A qué se dedica tu amigo? or ¿Qué es tu hermana? — What does your friend do? / What does your sister do?

Mixed groups go masculine

A plural with at least one man uses the masculine form: Ana y Pedro son abogados. Only an all-female group takes abogadas.

Jobs Ending in -o

The largest group of job words ends in -o in the masculine. For a woman, change the -o to -a. Add -s for the plural.

Masculine Feminine English
médico médica doctor
abogado abogada lawyer
maestro maestra (school) teacher
enfermero enfermera nurse
camarero camarera waiter / waitress
cocinero cocinera cook
arquitecto arquitecta architect
ingeniero ingeniera engineer
bombero bombera firefighter
cartero cartera mail carrier
mecánico mecánica mechanic
peluquero peluquera hairdresser
científico científica scientist
fotógrafo fotógrafa photographer
psicólogo psicóloga psychologist
  • Mi vecina es fotógrafa y su marido es cocinero. — My neighbour is a photographer and her husband is a cook.
  • ¿Tu padre es mecánico? — Is your father a mechanic?

A few -o words don't change

Some jobs keep -o for both genders and only the article shows gender: el piloto / la piloto, el modelo / la modelo. You'll also hear la soldado for a female soldier.

Jobs Ending in a Consonant

Many masculine job words end in a consonant, very often -or. To make the feminine, add -a. If the last syllable had a written accent, it disappears.

Masculine Feminine English
profesor profesora teacher
doctor doctora doctor
director directora director, head
pintor pintora painter
escritor escritora writer
traductor traductora translator
vendedor vendedora salesperson
programador programadora programmer
entrenador entrenadora coach, trainer
bailarín bailarina dancer
capitán capitana captain

The plurals are -es for men or mixed groups and -as for women: traductores, traductoras.

  • Soy programadora y trabajo en una empresa pequeña. — I'm a programmer and I work at a small company.
  • Los entrenadores son muy simpáticos. — The coaches are very nice.

Not every -or adds -a

A handful have their own feminine: actor → actriz, emperador → emperatriz. These are rare in everyday conversation.

Jobs That Don't Change

Some job words have one form for both genders. Only the article (el / la, un / una) and any adjectives show whether you mean a man or a woman.

Words ending in -ista: dentista, periodista, taxista, artista, electricista, recepcionista, florista, economista

Words ending in -e or -nte: estudiante, cantante, agente, detective, intérprete, ayudante, conserje

Some words ending in -a: atleta, poeta, policía, guía, astronauta, terapeuta, psiquiatra

Man Woman
el taxista la taxista
un estudiante una estudiante
el policía la policía
el guía turístico la guía turística
  • Mi hermana es periodista. — My sister is a journalist.
  • El dentista es nuevo, pero la recepcionista es la misma. — The dentist is new, but the receptionist is the same.

Language changes

As more women hold jobs once reserved for men, new feminine forms are appearing. La presidenta, la jefa (boss) and la clienta are now completely normal, even though presidente and jefe end in -e.

la policía has two meanings

La policía can be a policewoman or "the police" as an institution: Llamé a la policía. — I called the police.

Special Feminine Forms

A small group of job and title words form the feminine in an unexpected way, often with -esa, -isa or -ina. These simply have to be learned.

Masculine Feminine English
el alcalde la alcaldesa mayor
el dependiente la dependienta shop assistant
el actor la actriz actor / actress
el príncipe la princesa prince / princess
el duque la duquesa duke / duchess
el rey la reina king / queen
el héroe la heroína hero / heroine
el poeta la poeta or la poetisa poet
  • La alcaldesa visita la escuela hoy. — The mayor (a woman) is visiting the school today.
  • La dependienta es muy amable. — The shop assistant is very kind.

Putting it all together

Introduce a group of friends and their jobs in one go:

  • Te presento a mis amigos. Este es Raúl, es cocinero. — Let me introduce my friends. This is Raúl, he's a cook.
  • Esta es Inés; es actriz. — This is Inés; she's an actress.
  • Y estas son Bea y Sonia: son estudiantes de medicina. — And these are Bea and Sonia: they're medical students.

Learn the rule, then the exceptions

Most job words you meet will follow the -o / -a or consonant + -a pattern. Keep a short list of exceptions like actriz and alcaldesa and add to it as you find new ones.
