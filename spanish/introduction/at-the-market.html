Food at the Market

A mercado (market) is the heart of many Spanish-speaking towns. Start with a few basic food and drink words:

Spanish English Spanish English
el pan bread la leche milk
el café coffee el agua water
la manzana apple la naranja orange
la ensalada salad el queso cheese
el sándwich sandwich el huevo egg
la hamburguesa hamburger el helado ice cream

Every noun is masculine or feminine. As a rough guide, words ending in -o are usually masculine and words ending in -a usually feminine. Others (pan, leche, café) just have to be learned with their article.

  • El pan está muy rico. — The bread is really tasty.
  • La leche está en la nevera. — The milk is in the fridge.

el agua is feminine

Agua is a feminine word, but it takes el in the singular because it starts with a stressed a: el agua fría (cold water), las aguas.

Market stalls

At a market you buy from a puesto (stall) run by a vendedor or vendedora. Fruit is sold at the frutería, bread at the panadería and cheese at the quesería.

Un and Una: A, An and One

Spanish has two words for "a" or "an": un before masculine nouns and una before feminine ones. They also mean "one".

masculine feminine
un café — a coffee una manzana — an apple
un sándwich — a sandwich una ensalada — a salad
un helado — an ice cream una hamburguesa — a hamburger
  • Quiero un café y una manzana. — I want a coffee and an apple.
  • Tengo una naranja. — I have an orange / one orange.

Plural forms are unos and unas, meaning "some": unos huevos (some eggs), unas naranjas (some oranges).

With things you can't count, like milk, bread or water, Spanish usually uses no article at all:

  • Quiero leche. — I want (some) milk.
  • ¿Tiene pan? — Do you have (any) bread?

Learn nouns with un or una

Don't memorise manzana; memorise una manzana. Then you never have to guess the gender later.

Numbers One to Five

Prices and quantities need numbers. Start with the first five:

Number Spanish
1 uno (un / una before a noun)
2 dos
3 tres
4 cuatro
5 cinco

From two upwards, the noun goes plural, usually by adding -s (after a vowel) or -es (after a consonant):

  • un helado → dos helados — one ice cream → two ice creams
  • una manzana → tres manzanas — one apple → three apples
  • un pan → cuatro panes — one loaf → four loaves

uno café → un café; una pesos → un peso

More numbers

The next five are seis (6), siete (7), ocho (8), nueve (9) and diez (10). They never change for gender.

What Do You Want?

A stallholder or waiter often greets you with a question built on querer (to want). With a customer they normally use the formal usted form:

Spanish English
¿Qué quiere? What would you like? (formal)
¿Qué quieres? What do you want? (informal)
¿Quiere algo más? Would you like anything else?
¿Quiere helado? Do you want ice cream?

Querer is a "stem-changing" verb: the e in the middle becomes ie in most forms.

querer
yo quiero
tú quieres
usted, él, ella quiere
nosotros queremos
ustedes, ellos quieren
  • —Buenos días, ¿qué quiere? — Good morning, what would you like?
  • —Buenos días. Quiero pan, por favor. — Good morning. I'd like some bread, please.

Pronounce the qu as k

In quiero, qué and queso, the u is silent: say "KYEH-ro", not "KWEH-ro".

I Want…

To ask for something, use quiero + what you want. It's direct, but adding por favor makes it perfectly polite at a market.

  • Quiero un café, por favor. — I'd like a coffee, please.
  • Quiero una ensalada. — I'd like a salad.
  • Quiero dos manzanas y una naranja. — I'd like two apples and an orange.
  • Quiero pan y leche. — I'd like bread and milk.

Other polite ways to order, from more direct to more polite:

Spanish English
Quiero… I want…
Deme…, por favor. Give me…, please.
¿Me da…? Can I have…?
Quisiera… I would like…
Me gustaría… I'd like…

To say what you don't want, put no before the verb:

  • No quiero helado, quiero fruta. — I don't want ice cream, I want fruit.

Point and say "esto"

If you don't know a word, point and say Quiero esto, por favor — "I'd like this, please" — or eso for something further away.

How Much Does It Cost?

The verb costar (to cost) is another stem-changer: o becomes ue. At the market you mostly need two forms:

Spanish English
¿Cuánto cuesta? How much does it cost?
¿Cuánto cuestan? How much do they cost?
Cuesta… It costs…
Cuestan… They cost…

Use cuesta for one thing and cuestan for several:

  • ¿Cuánto cuesta el queso? — How much is the cheese?
  • ¿Cuánto cuestan las naranjas? — How much are the oranges?

Another very common way to ask is ¿Cuánto es? — "How much is it?" — especially when you're about to pay for everything.

  • —¿Cuánto es todo? —Son cuatro euros. — How much is everything? — It's four euros.

Cuánto needs its accent

As a question word, cuánto has an accent. And it means "how much", not "what": ¿Qué cuesta? → ¿Cuánto cuesta?

Prices in Pesos

Many Latin American countries use the peso (Mexico, Argentina, Colombia, Chile and others). Spain uses the euro; other countries have the sol (Peru), the colón (Costa Rica) or the US dólar.

To give a price, say cuesta + number + currency. After "one", the currency is singular; after the other numbers it's plural.

Spanish English
Cuesta un peso. It costs one peso.
Cuesta dos pesos. It costs two pesos.
Cuesta tres pesos. It costs three pesos.
Cuestan cuatro euros. They cost four euros.
Cuesta cinco dólares. It costs five dollars.
  • —¿Cuánto cuesta el helado? —Cuesta dos pesos. — How much is the ice cream? — Two pesos.
  • Las manzanas cuestan cinco pesos el kilo. — The apples are five pesos a kilo.

Bargaining

At traditional markets and craft stalls in Latin America, a little friendly bargaining is common: ¿Me lo deja en tres? — "Will you let me have it for three?" In supermarkets and in Spain, prices are fixed.

Do You Have…?

To ask whether a stall sells something, use tener (to have). It is irregular, so learn the forms by heart:

tener
yo tengo
tú tienes
usted, él, ella tiene
nosotros tenemos
ustedes, ellos tienen
  • ¿Tiene café? — Do you have coffee? (to the vendor, formal)
  • ¿Tienen huevos? — Do you (all / the shop) have eggs?
  • Sí, tengo leche. — Yes, I have milk.
  • No tenemos pan hoy. — We don't have bread today.

As a customer you'll also say what you have: Tengo cinco pesos. — I have five pesos. No tengo cambio. — I don't have change.

¿Hay…? works too

¿Hay pan? — "Is there bread?" — is another simple way to ask if something is available. Hay never changes: ¿Hay manzanas?

Yes and No Answers

Short answers at the market combine sí or no with a full sentence.

Yes:

  • —¿Quiere helado? —Sí, quiero helado. — Do you want ice cream? — Yes, I want ice cream.
  • —¿Tiene leche? —Sí, tengo leche. — Do you have milk? — Yes, I have milk.

No, plus a correction — here no is the answer, and what follows says what you want instead:

  • —¿Quiere un café? —No, quiero un sándwich. — Do you want a coffee? — No, I want a sandwich.

No, plus a negative verb — to say you don't want or have something, use no twice:

  • —¿Tiene pan? —No, no tengo pan. — Do you have bread? — No, I don't have bread.
Spanish English
Sí, por favor. Yes, please.
No, gracias. No, thank you.
Nada más, gracias. Nothing else, thanks.
Aquí tiene. Here you are.

sí vs. si

Sí with an accent means "yes". Si without it means "if": Si tienes pan, quiero uno. — If you have bread, I want one.

A full exchange

  • —Buenos días. ¿Qué quiere? — Good morning. What would you like?
  • —¿Tiene manzanas? — Do you have apples?
  • —Sí. ¿Cuántas quiere? — Yes. How many would you like?
  • —Tres, por favor. ¿Cuánto cuestan? — Three, please. How much are they?
  • —Cuestan tres pesos. ¿Algo más? — Three pesos. Anything else?
  • —No, gracias. Aquí tiene. — No, thanks. Here you are.
