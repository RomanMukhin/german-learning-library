Five Ways to Say You

English has one word for you. Spanish has five, and the right one depends on how many people you are talking to, how formal the relationship is, and where you are.

Pronoun Number Formality Where
tú one person informal almost everywhere
usted one person formal everywhere
vos one person informal (and often for everyone) Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, parts of Chile and Central America
vosotros / vosotras two or more informal Spain
ustedes two or more formal everywhere; also informal in Latin America everywhere

Each pronoun comes with its own verb form:

ser (to be) hablar (to speak) tener (to have)
tú eres hablas tienes
vos sos hablás tenés
usted es habla tiene
vosotros sois habláis tenéis
ustedes son hablan tienen

The pronoun usually disappears

Because the verb ending already shows who you mean, native speakers often leave the pronoun out: ¿Hablas inglés? rather than ¿Tú hablas inglés? The pronoun is added for emphasis, contrast or clarity (especially with usted).

Tú: Informal You

Use tú with one person you know well or who is on your level:

  • friends, classmates and close colleagues
  • family members, in most families
  • children and people younger than you
  • pets
  • most online chats and social media
  • ¿Vienes tú a la playa mañana? — Are you coming to the beach tomorrow?
  • ¿Cómo te llamas, guapo? — What's your name, sweetie? (to a child)
  • Tienes un perro muy bonito. — You have a really nice dog.

The tú verb form nearly always ends in -s: hablas, comes, vives, estás, tienes, quieres. Related words: te (you, object), ti (after prepositions: para ti), tu (your, no accent).

tú vs. tu

With an accent, tú means "you". Without it, tu means "your": ¿Tú tienes tu libro? — Do you have your book?

Usted: Formal You

Usted (written Ud. or Vd.) shows respect or distance. Use it with:

  • someone you don't know, especially an adult
  • older people
  • your boss, a teacher at university, officials, a doctor
  • customers and clients

Grammatically, usted takes the same verb form as él / ella (he / she), even though it means "you":

  • ¿Cómo está usted? — How are you?
  • Usted tiene razón. — You're right.
  • ¿De dónde es usted, señora? — Where are you from, madam?
  • Perdone, ¿habla usted inglés? — Excuse me, do you speak English?

Related forms: se in ¿Cómo se llama?, le (to you), and su (your): ¿Es su coche? — Is this your car?

When in doubt, start with usted

Using usted with someone who expected tú is a small, polite mistake; the other way round can seem rude. Start formal and let the other person invite you to relax.

In some places, such as Colombia and Costa Rica, usted is also used between friends and family, so you will hear it in warm, informal contexts too.

Vos: The Southern You

In Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay, and widely in Central America, vos replaces tú. It's not old-fashioned or rare there — it's simply how people speak, from the street to TV.

The present-tense vos forms usually move the stress to the end and add an accent:

Verb tú vos
hablar hablas hablás
comer comes comés
vivir vives vivís
ser eres sos
querer quieres querés
poder puedes podés
  • ¿Vos sos de Buenos Aires? — Are you from Buenos Aires?
  • ¿Querés un mate? — Do you want some mate?
  • Te quiero mucho a vos. — I love you a lot.

The object pronoun and the possessive stay the same as with tú: te and tu (¿Cómo te llamás? ¿Es tu casa?).

You only need to recognise it

If you learn tú, people in vos countries will understand you perfectly. Just learn to recognise forms like sos, tenés and querés when you hear them.

Vosotros and Ustedes: Plural You

When you speak to two or more people, the choice depends mainly on geography.

In Spain: vosotros (all-male or mixed group) and vosotras (all-female group) for friends and family; ustedes for formal groups.

  • ¿Adónde vais vosotros esta noche? — Where are you guys going tonight?
  • Chicas, ¿queréis un café? — Girls, do you want a coffee?
  • Señores, ¿ustedes tienen reserva? — Gentlemen, do you have a booking?

In Latin America: ustedes for every group, formal or not. Vosotros is not used in everyday speech.

  • Hola, chicos, ¿quieren ustedes ir al cine? — Hi, guys, do you want to go to the cinema?
  • Doctor Gómez y doctora Ríos, ¿ustedes tienen un momento? — Dr Gómez and Dr Ríos, do you have a moment?
Spain Latin America
friends vosotros habláis ustedes hablan
formal ustedes hablan ustedes hablan

Like usted, ustedes uses the third-person verb form (ellos / ellas): ustedes son, ustedes tienen. Possessive: su / sus; for vosotros: vuestro, vuestra.

Dropping Formality: Tutear

The verb tutear means "to address someone as tú". Switching from usted to tú is a small social step, and often the older or more senior person offers it.

Spanish English
¿Nos tuteamos? Shall we use tú?
Puedes tutearme. You can call me tú.
Háblame de tú, por favor. Please use tú with me.
No me trates de usted. Don't use usted with me.
  • —¿Cómo está usted, señora Pérez? —Bien, gracias, pero puedes tutearme. Me llamo Carmen. — How are you, Mrs Pérez? — Fine, thanks, but you can use tú. My name is Carmen.

The opposite verb is ustedear (to use usted), heard mainly in Latin America.

Mirror the other person

If you can't decide, listen to which form the other person uses with you and match it. When someone uses tú with you, you can usually answer the same way — unless they are clearly much older or senior.

Keep the verb consistent

Once you choose usted, keep its verb forms and pronouns throughout: Usted tienes → Usted tiene; ¿Cómo te llama usted? → ¿Cómo se llama usted?
