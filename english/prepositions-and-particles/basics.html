Prepositions and particles

Prepositions: the basics

What prepositions do, how they differ from adverbs and conjunctions, where they go, and how prepositional phrases are built.

Prepositions

Uses

Prepositions express relationships, mostly of place (in the box, under the bed), direction (to the shops, into the room), time (at 5, on Monday, during the film) and many abstract meanings (about the war, for my mother, because of the rain).

Most are single words (at, by, with); some are complex: because of, in front of, according to, instead of, in spite of, next to.

Prepositions or conjunctions?

A preposition is followed by a noun phrase, pronoun or -ing form; a conjunction is followed by a clause. Some words are both:

  • after lunch (preposition) / after we had lunch (conjunction)
  • because of the rain (preposition) / because it was raining (conjunction)

Prepositions or adverbs?

Many prepositions can also be adverbs, with no noun after them:

  • She ran out of the house. (preposition) / She ran out. (adverb)
  • Come in! / Have you been here before?

Prepositions with adjectives, nouns and verbs

Many words are followed by a fixed preposition, which must be learnt with the word:

Adjective + prep Noun + prep Verb + prep
afraid of, good at, interested in, proud of, keen on, responsible for reason for, interest in, solution to, advantage of, increase in depend on, listen to, belong to, wait for, apply for, consist of

Position and stranding

Prepositions normally come before their complement. In questions, relative clauses, passives and some infinitive structures, they can go at the end (be "stranded"):

  • Who are you talking to? (formal: To whom are you talking?)
  • That's the flat (that) I told you about.
  • She hates being laughed at.
  • It's a nice place to live in.

Prepositional phrases

Prepositions and their complements

A prepositional phrase is a preposition + its complement. The complement can be:

  • a noun phrase: on the table
  • a pronoun: with her
  • an -ing clause: before leaving
  • a wh-clause: about what happened
  • an adverb: until recently, from here

After prepositions, use object pronouns (for me, not for I) and -ing, not the infinitive: interested in learning, not in to learn.

A prepositional phrase can be modified: right behind you, just after midnight, straight into the wall.

Prepositional phrases after verbs

They can act as adverbials (She lives in Paris), as complements of linking verbs (He's in a bad mood), or after nouns as postmodifiers (the girl with the red hair).

At, in and to (movement)

To talk about movement towards a place we use to: I'm going to London. She walked to the door.

When we arrive, we use arrive at for small places, buildings and points, and arrive in for cities and countries: arrive at the station / the airport, arrive in Paris / Japan. Never arrive to.

  • Get to work / go to school, but go home (no to)
  • Into emphasises entering: She walked into the room.

At, on and in (place)

Use Examples
at a point, a building as a place for an activity, events, addresses with a number at the bus stop, at the door, at school, at a party, at 15 Hill Street
on a surface, a line, floors, public transport, streets (British) on the table, on the wall, on the river, on the second floor, on the bus, on Oxford Street
in an enclosed space, an area, cities, countries in the box, in the kitchen, in the park, in London, in France
  • She's at work. (place of activity) / There's a mouse in the office. (inside)
  • in a car / taxi, but on a bus / train / plane / bike
  • in the picture, on the page, at the top / bottom of the page

At, on and in (time)

Use Examples
at clock times, festivals, short periods at 6 o'clock, at noon, at night, at the weekend (Br), at Christmas
on days and dates on Monday, on 5 May, on my birthday, on Christmas Day, on Friday morning
in longer periods: months, years, seasons, parts of the day in July, in 2010, in winter, in the morning, in the 1990s

At the end or in the end?

At the end (of) = at the final point: at the end of the film. In the end = finally, after a long time or many changes: In the end, we stayed home.

At the beginning or in the beginning?

At the beginning (of) = at the start of something: at the beginning of May. In the beginning = at first, in contrast with later.

Other uses of in with time

In can mean "after a period from now": I'll be back in ten minutes. It also shows how long something takes: I did it in an hour.

No preposition

Don't use at, on, in before this, next, last, every, each, all, some, one, tomorrow, yesterday: I'll see you next Friday, not on next Friday.

Typical errors

  • in Monday → on Monday; at the morning → in the morning; in the night (usually) → at night
