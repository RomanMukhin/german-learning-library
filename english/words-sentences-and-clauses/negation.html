Neither, neither … nor and not … either

Neither as a determiner and pronoun

Neither + singular noun = not one and not the other (of two): Neither answer is correct. Neither of them came.

Neither … nor

Neither … nor joins two negative alternatives: Neither Tom nor Anna was there. She neither smokes nor drinks. Don't add not: Neither Tom nor Anna wasn't there.

Neither do I, nor can she

To agree with a negative statement: "I don't like it." "Neither / Nor do I." "She can't swim." "Neither can I."

Not … either

Not … either is more informal and goes at the end: I don't like it either. "I'm not hungry." "I'm not either." / "Me neither." (very informal)

Typical errors

  • I don't like it too. → I don't like it either.
  • Neither of them didn't come. → Neither of them came.

Not

In negative statements

Not (or n't) goes after the first auxiliary or modal, or after be: She hasn't finished. I did not know. He isn't here. Without an auxiliary, use do: I don't like it.

In questions

Didn't you hear? (common) / Did you not hear? (more formal or emphatic)

Orders and instructions

Don't worry. Do not enter. (formal, notices)

Short replies, contrast and other uses

  • short replies: "Is it raining?" "I hope not." "Are you coming?" "Not today."
  • contrast: I asked for tea, not coffee.
  • not + -ing / to-infinitive: Not knowing what to do, I called her. Try not to be late.
  • or not: Are you coming or not?

Forming negative statements, questions and imperatives

Negative statements

  • auxiliary / modal / be + not: I haven't seen it. You can't go. She isn't ready.
  • no auxiliary → do / does / did + not + base form: He doesn't work here.

Negative questions

Negative questions show surprise, ask for confirmation, or criticise: Haven't you finished yet? Don't you like it? Wouldn't it be better to wait? Answers refer to the fact: "Aren't you coming?" "No, I'm not." / "Yes, I am."

Negative imperatives

Don't + base form: Don't shout. Don't be late. Let's not for suggestions: Let's not argue.

Negation: two negatives

Standard English uses only one negative in a clause: I didn't do nothing → I didn't do anything / I did nothing. Two negatives may cancel each other out: I can't not go (= I must go).

Not … I don't think

With think, believe, suppose, expect, the negative usually goes on the reporting verb: I don't think she's coming (more natural than I think she isn't coming). Don't put the negative in both places: I don't think she isn't coming.

Negative clauses with any, anybody, anyone, anything, anywhere

After not, use any-words, not no-words: I didn't see anybody. There isn't any milk. She doesn't go anywhere. = I saw nobody. / There's no milk. (no-words are a little more emphatic or formal)

At the start of a clause, only no-words are possible: Nobody came. Not: Anybody didn't come.

Negation in non-finite clauses

In infinitive and -ing clauses, not goes before the verb form:

  • I decided not to go. Not: I decided to not go (common informally, but avoid in formal writing)
  • She was angry at not being invited.
  • Try not to worry.
  • Not wanting to wake him, I left quietly.

Negative prefixes and suffixes

Many adjectives, verbs and nouns are made negative with prefixes or suffixes:

Affix Examples
un- unhappy, unusual, undo, unlock
in- / im- / il- / ir- incorrect, impossible, illegal, irregular
dis- dishonest, disagree, dislike
non- non-smoker, non-fiction
mis- misunderstand (wrongly)
anti- anti-social
-less careless, useless, homeless

A negative-prefix word with not can make a careful, understated meaning: It's not unusual (= it's fairly common).

Negative adverbs: hardly, seldom, etc.

Adverbs such as hardly, scarcely, barely, seldom, rarely, never have a negative meaning, so they are not used with not:

  • I can hardly see. Not: I can't hardly see
  • We rarely go out.

With these words, use any-forms and positive tags: There was hardly anybody there, was there? At the front of a sentence (formal), they cause inversion: Seldom have I seen such a mess.

Negation: emphasising

To make a negative stronger, use:

  • at all: I don't like it at all.
  • whatsoever: I have no idea whatsoever.
  • not … a single / not one: I didn't see a single person. Not one student passed.
  • by no means / in no way / not in the least / not a bit: It's by no means easy. I'm not in the least surprised.
  • never ever (informal), absolutely not, certainly not

Negation of think, believe, suppose, hope

With think, believe, suppose, expect, imagine, we usually make the verb negative, not the clause:

  • I don't think it will rain. (more natural than I think it won't rain)
  • I don't believe we've met.

Hope is different: the clause is made negative: I hope it doesn't rain. Not: I don't hope it rains. In short answers: I think so / I don't think so, I hope not, I suppose not / I don't suppose so.
