Word classes and phrase classes

Major word classes

The four major (open) word classes are nouns (table, idea), verbs (run, think), adjectives (big, happy) and adverbs (quickly, often). New words are added to them all the time.

Typical word-class suffixes

  • nouns: -tion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -er: information, movement, kindness
  • verbs: -ise/-ize, -ify, -en: realise, simplify, widen
  • adjectives: -ful, -less, -ous, -able, -al, -ive: careful, famous, natural
  • adverbs: -ly: quickly

Other word classes

The minor (closed) classes rarely get new members: prepositions (in, on, with), pronouns (she, which), determiners (the, some), conjunctions (and, because) and interjections (oh, wow).

Phrase classes

Words combine into phrases named after their main word: noun phrase (the old car), verb phrase (has been sleeping), adjective phrase (very tired), adverb phrase (quite slowly) and prepositional phrase (in the morning).

Word formation

English makes new words in several ways:

  • prefixes: happy → unhappy
  • suffixes: happy → happiness
  • conversion (same form, new class): a text → to text, to google, an email
  • compounding: sun + glasses → sunglasses
  • abbreviation: advertisement → ad, British Broadcasting Corporation → BBC
  • back-formation (removing a suffix): editor → edit, television → televise
  • blending: breakfast + lunch → brunch, smoke + fog → smog
  • loan words from other languages: café, kindergarten, karaoke, tsunami
  • new words for new things: selfie, podcast, emoji

Prefixes

Writing prefixes: hyphens

Most prefixes join the word directly (supermodel, rewrite). Use a hyphen before a capital letter (anti-American), with ex- and self- (ex-boss, self-confident), to avoid double vowels (co-owner, re-enter), or to avoid confusion (re-cover = cover again / recover = get better).

Suffixes

Spelling with suffixes

Final -e is usually dropped before a vowel suffix (create → creative), y changes to i (happy → happiness), and consonants may double (begin → beginner).

Common suffixes

Class Suffixes Examples
noun -er/-or, -ist, -ian, -ment, -tion, -ness, -ity, -ship, -hood, -dom, -ism, -ance/-ence teacher, artist, musician, payment, education, kindness, reality, friendship, childhood, freedom, tourism, difference
adjective -able/-ible, -al, -ful, -less, -ic, -ive, -ous, -y, -ish readable, national, helpful, useless, artistic, active, famous, sunny, foolish
verb -ise/-ize, -ify, -en, -ate modernise, purify, darken, activate
adverb -ly, -wards, -wise slowly, backwards, clockwise

Compounds

Compound words

Compounds are made of two or more words that act as one unit.

  • nouns: bookshop, washing machine, mother-in-law
  • adjectives: well-known, good-looking, blue-eyed, world-famous, two-hour (a two-hour film)
  • verbs: babysit, download, overtake, dry-clean

Writing compound words

They may be written as one word (bedroom), two words (post office) or with a hyphen (part-time). Compound adjectives before a noun are usually hyphenated: a well-known writer (but The writer is well known).

Compound sentences

Don't confuse compound words with compound sentences, which join two main clauses: I came and I saw.

Abbreviations, initials and acronyms

Abbreviations and clipping

Clipping shortens a word: advertisement → ad, examination → exam, refrigerator → fridge, telephone → phone, influenza → flu. Written abbreviations: Mr, Dr, St, etc., e.g., i.e., approx.

Personal names

Names are often shortened, sometimes with a -y / -ie ending: Robert → Rob, Bob, Bobby; Elizabeth → Liz, Beth, Lizzie; William → Will, Bill.

Initials

Initials are pronounced as letters: BBC, USA, UK, EU, CEO, DIY. Use a / an according to the first sound: an MP, a UFO.

Acronyms

Acronyms are pronounced as words: NATO, UNESCO, laser, scuba, AIDS.

-ish and -y

The suffix -ish means "quite, rather, approximately": reddish (rather red), tallish, fiftyish, at sevenish. Informally, -ish can stand alone: "Are you hungry?" "Ish." With nationalities and nouns it means "typical of": Spanish, childish (negative), foolish. The suffix -y makes adjectives from nouns, meaning "full of, like": sunny, rainy, salty, sleepy, greeny-blue.

Diminutives (-let, -y and mini-)

Diminutives show something is small or used affectionately:

  • -let: booklet, piglet, droplet, leaflet
  • -y / -ie: doggy, birdie, sweetie, Johnny (often for children or affection)
  • mini-: minibus, miniskirt, mini-break
  • others: -ette (kitchenette), -ling (duckling)

Hyphens

Hyphens join words or parts of words:

  • compound adjectives before a noun: a well-paid job, a three-year-old girl
  • numbers 21–99 and fractions: twenty-five, two-thirds
  • some prefixes: ex-, self-, all-, anti- before capitals: self-control, pre-1990
  • to avoid confusion or awkward letter combinations: re-sign (sign again) / resign
  • at the end of a line to split a word

The use of hyphens in compounds is changing: many become single words over time (e-mail → email).
