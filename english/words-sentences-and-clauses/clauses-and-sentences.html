Adjuncts

Adjuncts (adverbials) add optional information about time, place, manner, reason and so on. If you remove them, the clause is still complete: She sang beautifully at the concert last night.

Adjuncts and complements

Complements are needed to complete the meaning of a verb: She put the book on the shelf (can't remove). Adjuncts are extra: She read the book on the train.

Adjuncts and postmodifiers in noun phrases

The man in the car was talking (postmodifier — describes the man) vs The man was talking in the car (adjunct — tells us where he was talking).

Clauses

Introduction

A clause is a group of words with a verb, usually with a subject: She laughed. Because it was late…

Main and subordinate clauses

A main (independent) clause can stand alone: I went home. A subordinate (dependent) clause cannot; it depends on a main clause: I went home because I was tired.

Coordinated clauses

Two main clauses joined by and, but, or, so are coordinated: I called and she answered.

Clauses: finite and non-finite

Finite clauses

They have a finite verb with tense: She works here. If it rains…

Non-finite clauses

They contain a non-finite verb form (infinitive, -ing, past participle) and usually no subject:

  • To be honest, I don't like it.
  • Walking home, I met Tom.
  • Built in 1900, the house is now a museum.

After conjunctions, verbs and in relative clauses

  • after after, although, though, if, when, while: While waiting, I read a book. If necessary, call me.
  • after verbs: I want to go. I enjoy cooking.
  • reduced relative clauses: the man sitting there, the car parked outside

Clause types

  • declarative (statements): She is here.
  • interrogative (questions): Is she here? Where is she?
  • imperative (commands, requests, invitations): Come in. Have a seat. You stay here! Do sit down. Let's go.
  • exclamative: What a mess! How strange!

Complements

Subject and object complements

  • subject complements describe the subject after a linking verb: She is a doctor. He seems tired.
  • object complements describe the object: They elected her president. You make me happy.

Other verbs need complements to complete their meaning: She put it on the table. He's interested in art.

Complements and adjuncts

Complements are necessary; adjuncts are optional (see Adjuncts).

Dummy subjects

English clauses need a subject, so when there is no real one we use it or there.

It

For weather, time, distance and to anticipate a clause: It's raining. It's late. It's easy to see why.

There

There + be introduces the existence of something, usually new information: There is a problem. There are two cats in the garden. The verb agrees with the noun after it: There are many reasons. Not: It has many reasons (as in some languages).

Exclamations

  • What + (adjective) + noun: What a beautiful day! What nice people! (no a with plurals and uncountables: What awful weather!)
  • How + adjective / adverb: How kind! How quickly she learns!
  • question form (with falling intonation): Isn't it lovely! Wasn't that amazing!
  • so / such: It's so hot! She's such a star!

Heads

The head is the main word of a phrase, which decides the phrase's type and its grammar: in the tall man in the hat, the head is man (a noun phrase); in very happy, it's happy (an adjective phrase). The verb agrees with the head of the subject: The colour of the walls is green.

Objects

Direct objects

The direct object is the person or thing affected by the action: She bought a car.

Indirect objects

The indirect object is the receiver, usually a person, and comes before the direct object: She gave me a present. With to / for it comes after: She gave a present to me.

Verbs and objects

Transitive verbs need an object (She enjoyed it); intransitive verbs don't (He arrived). Some verbs have two objects (give, send, show, tell).

No objects with linking verbs

Linking verbs (be, seem, become) take complements, not objects: She became a doctor (complement).

Sentences

Sentence structures

  • simple sentence: one main clause: I like tea.
  • compound sentence: two or more main clauses joined by a coordinator: I like tea, but she likes coffee.
  • complex sentence: a main clause + one or more subordinate clauses: I like tea because it's relaxing.

Types of sentence

Statements, questions, commands and exclamations (see Clause types). In writing, a sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark.

Subjects

Position

The subject normally comes before the verb in statements and after the auxiliary in questions: She lives here. Does she live here?

Dummy subjects and no subject

English needs a subject even when the meaning is empty: It's cold. There is a bus. Only imperatives and some informal ellipsis have no subject: Sit down. (I) Hope you're well.

Typical errors

  • Is raining. → It is raining.
  • Is a good idea. → It's a good idea.
  • Has many people here. → There are many people here.

Subject complements

Parts of speech

Subject complements can be adjective phrases (She is very clever), noun phrases (He became a teacher), prepositional phrases (They're in the garden) or clauses (The problem is that we're late).

Pronouns as subject complements

Informal: It's me. That's him. Formal: It is I. (rare and very formal)

Subject–verb agreement

The verb agrees with its subject in number:

  • singular subject → singular verb: The car is new. She works.
  • plural subject → plural verb: The cars are new.
  • the head noun decides: The list of names is long.
  • everyone, everybody, nobody, each + singular verb: Everyone is here.
  • uncountable nouns + singular verb: The information is correct.
  • collective nouns can take singular or plural in British English: The team is / are winning.
  • two subjects joined by and = plural: Tom and Anna are here. With or / nor, the verb agrees with the nearer one.
  • amounts as one unit = singular: Ten pounds is too much.
