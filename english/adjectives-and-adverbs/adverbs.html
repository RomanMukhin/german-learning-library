Adverbs

Adverbs and adverb phrases tell us more about a verb, an adjective, another adverb or a whole clause. They answer questions such as how?, when?, where?, how often? and how much?.

  • She spoke quietly. (verb)
  • It was really good. (adjective)
  • He drives very fast. (adverb)
  • Fortunately, we arrived on time. (whole clause)

Meanings and functions

Adverbs can describe manner (slowly), place (here), time (yesterday), frequency (often), degree (extremely), give the speaker's attitude (honestly, sadly), focus on one part of a sentence (only, even) or link ideas (however, then).

Adverb phrases

An adverb phrase has an adverb as its main word, sometimes with words before or after it: very slowly, quite often, luckily for us, more quickly than expected.

Types and functions

Adverb phrases can modify:

  • a verb: She sang beautifully.
  • be: He is always late.
  • an adjective or adverb: incredibly expensive, far too quickly
  • another phrase: right behind the door, just after midnight
  • a determiner or number: almost all, nearly twenty

Prepositional phrases (in the morning, with care) and some noun phrases (last week, this way) often do the same job as adverbs.

Adverbs and adverb phrases: position

Adverbs can go in three main positions:

Position Where Example
front before the subject Suddenly, the lights went out.
mid between subject and main verb, after be or the first auxiliary She often works late. / He is never on time. / I have just finished.
end after the verb or the object They played well. / I'll see you tomorrow.

Manner, place and time

These normally go at the end. When there are several, the usual order is manner → place → time:

  • She sang beautifully at the concert last night.
  • Not: She sang last night beautifully.

Don't put an adverb between a verb and its object: She speaks English well, not She speaks well English.

Frequency, degree and focusing adverbs

These usually take mid position: I usually walk to work, We've almost finished, She only wanted to help.

Evaluative and viewpoint adverbs

These comment on the whole sentence and usually go at the front: Surprisingly, nobody complained. Personally, I prefer tea.

Here and there

After here and there at the front, a noun subject comes after the verb, but a pronoun subject does not: Here comes the bus. / Here it comes.

Adverbs and adverb phrases: typical errors

  • Adverb between verb and object: I like very much football. → I like football very much.
  • Adjective instead of adverb: She drives careful. → She drives carefully.
  • Mid-position after be: She always is late. → She is always late.
  • Time before place: We met yesterday in the park. (possible, but neutral order is) → We met in the park yesterday.

Adverbs: forms

Adverbs ending in -ly

Many adverbs are formed by adding -ly to an adjective:

Adjective Rule Adverb
quick, sad + ly quickly, sadly
happy, easy y → ily happily, easily
gentle, simple le → ly gently, simply
basic, tragic ic → ically basically, tragically (but publicly)
full + y fully

Adverbs ending in -ward(s) and -wise

backward(s), forward(s), upward(s), homeward(s) show direction; clockwise, otherwise, likewise show manner. Backwards is more common in British English, backward in American English.

Adverbs with the same form as adjectives

Some words are both adjectives and adverbs: fast, hard, late, early, straight, high, low, daily, long, well.

  • a fast car / She runs fast.
  • a hard exam / She works hard.

Some have a second, -ly form with a different meaning:

  • hard (with effort) / hardly (almost not)
  • late (not on time) / lately (recently)
  • near (close) / nearly (almost)
  • high (height) / highly (very: highly recommended)

Adjectives ending in -ly

friendly, lovely, lonely, silly, ugly, likely, costly are adjectives, not adverbs. Use a phrase instead: She spoke in a friendly way, not She spoke friendly.

Adverbs not related to adjectives

Many common adverbs have no adjective form: very, quite, soon, often, here, there, now, then, always, never, just, still.

Gradable adverbs

Adverbs of manner can usually be graded: very slowly, more slowly, too slowly.

Adverbs: functions

Adverbs can modify words, phrases and whole sentences:

  • verbs: He slowly opened the door.
  • adjectives: extremely hot, fairly easy
  • adverbs: quite often, very well
  • prepositional phrases: right into the room, straight after lunch
  • determiners and numbers: nearly everyone, about fifty people
  • nouns (a few adverbs): the very idea, the meeting yesterday
  • clauses: Clearly, he's not coming.

They can also act as complements of the verb be: The children are outside.

Adverbs: types

Time, place and manner (early, there, slowly)

  • Time — when or how long: now, soon, yesterday, recently, already, still, for ages
  • Place — where: here, there, upstairs, abroad, nearby, everywhere
  • Manner — how: slowly, carefully, well, badly, by hand

Frequency

always, usually, often, sometimes, occasionally, rarely, seldom, never, every day, once a week. Single-word frequency adverbs usually take mid position; phrases like every day go at the end.

Degree (slightly) and focusing (generally)

Degree adverbs say how much: very, extremely, quite, rather, slightly, completely, almost. Focusing adverbs point to one part of the sentence: only, just, even, also, especially, mainly, particularly, generally.

Evaluative (surprisingly) and viewpoint (personally)

Evaluative adverbs give the speaker's opinion: luckily, sadly, obviously, surprisingly, hopefully. Viewpoint adverbs say from which angle something is seen: personally, technically, financially, medically speaking.

Linking adverbs (then, however)

They connect clauses and sentences: however, therefore, also, then, besides, nevertheless, meanwhile, anyway.

  • It was late. However, we decided to go on.

Comparison: adverbs (worse, more easily)

Forms

Adverb Comparative Superlative
same as adjective: fast, hard, late, early faster, harder (the) fastest, hardest
-ly adverbs: carefully, easily more carefully, more easily (the) most carefully
well better (the) best
badly worse (the) worst
far further / farther (the) furthest / farthest
much / little more / less (the) most / least
  • She runs faster than her brother.
  • Could you speak more slowly, please? Not: slowlier
  • Who sings the best?

With adverbs, the before the superlative is often left out: Who sang best?

Fairly

Fairly is a weak degree adverb meaning "to some degree, but not very". It goes before gradable adjectives and adverbs and usually sounds less positive than quite or pretty.

  • The film was fairly good. (OK, not great)
  • I know him fairly well.

It goes after a/an: a fairly easy test. Fairly is not normally used with comparatives (use rather or a bit).

Intensifiers (very, at all)

Intensifiers make the meaning of a word stronger or weaker.

Strengthen Weaken
very, really, extremely, terribly, so, incredibly a bit, a little, slightly, fairly, rather, quite
with extreme adjectives: absolutely, completely, totally, utterly

At all strengthens negatives and questions: I didn't like it at all. Do you know him at all? Not at all is also a polite reply to thank you (British English).

Match the intensifier to the adjective

Gradable adjective → very: very good, very tired. Extreme adjective → absolutely: absolutely wonderful, absolutely exhausted. Really fits both.

Largely

Largely means "mainly" or "to a great extent". It is common in more formal speech and writing.

  • The success of the project was largely due to her work.
  • The audience was largely made up of students.

It does not mean "in a large way": The house was built largely → The house was big.

Much, a lot, lots, a good deal: adverbs

Much

As an adverb, much is mostly used in negatives and questions: I don't go out much. Does it hurt much? In affirmative sentences, a lot sounds more natural: I go out a lot, not I go out much.

Much strengthens comparatives and too: much better, much too expensive.

Very much

Very much is common in affirmative sentences with verbs like like, enjoy, appreciate, thank: I enjoyed it very much. Thank you very much. It goes after the object: I like Italy very much, not I very much like (possible but formal).

Too much, so much

I've eaten too much. I miss you so much.

A lot, lots, a good deal, a great deal

A lot and lots are informal; a good deal and a great deal are more formal.

  • She travels a lot.
  • It's lots easier now. (informal)
  • Prices have risen a great deal.

Pretty

In informal speech, pretty is a degree adverb meaning "quite" or "fairly": The test was pretty hard. She did pretty well. Pretty much means "almost": I've pretty much finished.

Don't confuse it with the adjective pretty (= attractive).

Quite

Quite + gradable adjectives and adverbs

With gradable words, quite in British English usually means "fairly, but not very":

  • The restaurant was quite good. (OK, but not excellent)

Stress and intonation matter: a falling tone on quite makes it weaker, a stress on the adjective makes it more positive.

Quite + non-gradable adjectives

With extreme or absolute words, quite means "completely": quite right, quite impossible, quite amazing.

Quite + nouns, a bit, a few, a lot

Quite goes before a/an: It was quite a good film. She's quite a character. Quite a few and quite a lot mean "a fairly large number/amount": There were quite a few people there.

Quite + verbs and comparatives

I quite like it (= I like it a bit). With comparatives, use quite a lot / quite a bit: She's quite a lot older than me, not quite older.

Not quite

Not quite means "not completely": I'm not quite ready. That's not quite what I meant.

Rather

Rather as a degree adverb

Rather is a little stronger than quite and is often used for things that are surprising or not wanted: It's rather cold today. The test was rather difficult. With positive words it can show pleasant surprise: The film was rather good.

Rather with nouns and comparatives

Rather can come before or after a: a rather long walk / rather a long walk. Unlike quite, it works with comparatives: It's rather better than I expected. Rather a lot means "quite a lot".

Rather + verb

I rather like it. I rather think you're right. (polite, British)

Rather than, would rather, or rather

  • I'll have tea rather than coffee. (alternatives)
  • I'd rather stay at home tonight. (preference)
  • He lives in London, or rather, just outside it. (correcting yourself)

Really

Really is very common in speech. It can:

  • strengthen adjectives and adverbs: It's really hot. She sings really well.
  • emphasise a verb: I really want to go.
  • mean "in fact": Is she really 40?
  • show interest or surprise as a response: "I'm moving to Japan." "Really?"

Position changes meaning: I don't really like it (= I don't like it much) vs. I really don't like it (= I strongly dislike it).

Scarcely

Scarcely means "almost not" or "only just". It is more formal than hardly.

  • I could scarcely believe my eyes.
  • There was scarcely any food left.

Scarcely … when describes one event happening immediately after another: We had scarcely sat down when the phone rang. In formal writing it can come first with inversion: Scarcely had we sat down when…

Very

Very before adjectives and adverbs

Very strengthens gradable adjectives and adverbs: very tall, very quickly.

When not to use very

  • Not with extreme or ungradable adjectives: very excellent → absolutely excellent
  • Not with comparatives: very better → much better
  • Not with verbs: I very like it → I like it very much
  • Not normally with -ed forms used as passive verbs: He was very admired → He was much / greatly admired (but very tired, very interested are fine, because they work as adjectives)

Very can go before best, first, last to emphasise: the very best, the very first time.

Not very

Not very is a polite way to say something negative: It's not very good (= quite bad).

About

About as a preposition

It means "on the subject of": a book about history, What are you talking about?

About as an adverb

It means "approximately": about 30 people, at about six o'clock. In British English it can also mean "in different places": There were clothes lying about.

Be about to

Be about to + infinitive means something will happen very soon: Hurry, the train is about to leave.

Ago

Ago shows how far back in the past something happened, counting from now. It comes after the time expression and is used with the past simple.

  • I met her three years ago.
  • How long ago did it happen?
  • Not: I have met her three years ago. / ago three years

When counting back from a moment in the past, use before or earlier: She had left two days before.

Already

Meaning

Already shows something has happened earlier than expected, or sooner than now: I've already eaten. In questions it shows surprise: Have you finished already?

Position

  • mid position (most common): She has already left.
  • end position (emphasis or surprise): Are you leaving already?
  • front position (rare): Already, the first guests were arriving.

Typical errors

British English normally uses the present perfect with already: I've already seen it (American English also allows I already saw it). Don't confuse already with all ready (= completely prepared).

Always

Meaning and position

Always means "every time" or "all the time". It goes in mid position: I always walk to work. She's always cheerful.

Always with continuous forms

With the present or past continuous, always often shows irritation: He's always losing his keys!

Commands and modals

In instructions, always goes first: Always lock the door. With can/could it suggests another possibility: If it rains, we can always go to the cinema.

As always, for ever, all the time

As always = as usual. Always means repeatedly; for ever / for good means permanently: She's left for good. All the time stresses frequency in a more informal or negative way: It rains all the time here.

Early

Early is both an adjective and an adverb: an early train, I got up early. There is no form earlily.

  • Not: I arrived soon to the meeting. → I arrived early for the meeting.

The opposite of early is late.

Ever

Ever: at any time

Ever means "at any time" and is used mainly in questions, negatives and after if and superlatives:

  • Have you ever been to Scotland?
  • Nobody ever calls me.
  • If you ever need help, call me.
  • It's the best film I've ever seen.

It is not used in affirmative statements: I have ever been to Rome.

Ever so, ever since, as … as ever

  • ever so (informal British) = very: That's ever so kind.
  • ever since = from then until now: I've lived here ever since.
  • as … as ever = as before: She's as busy as ever.

Hardly ever, rarely, scarcely, seldom

These frequency adverbs mean "not very often". Rarely and seldom are more formal than hardly ever. Scarcely ever is formal too.

  • I hardly ever watch TV.
  • We rarely see each other now.

Hardly and scarcely alone mean "almost not": I can hardly hear you.

Word order

They take mid position. In formal writing they can come at the front, followed by inversion: Rarely have I seen such a mess. Because they are already negative, don't add not: I don't hardly ever.

Next

Next as an adjective

Next week, next year, next Monday (without the) count from now. The next week means "the seven days after a particular time".

  • I'm seeing her next Friday. Not: the next Friday
  • It rained for the next three days. (after a past moment)

Next as adverb, linking word and pronoun

  • adverb: What should I do next?
  • linking: First, wash the rice. Next, add the water.
  • pronoun: Who's next?

Next to

Next to means "beside": She sat next to me.

No longer, not any longer

Both mean something that was true is not true now. No longer is more formal and goes in mid position; not … any longer is more common in speech and goes at the end.

  • She no longer works here.
  • She doesn't work here any longer.

Any more means the same: She doesn't work here any more.

No more, not any more

As determiners

No more / not any more + noun = no further amount: There's no more milk. / There isn't any more milk.

As adverbs

They mean "no longer": I don't smoke any more. No more as an adverb is formal or literary.

No more … than

No more … than means "equally not": He's no more a doctor than I am (= he's not a doctor).

Any more or anymore?

British English prefers two words, any more; American English usually writes anymore for the time meaning.

Now

Now as an adverb of time

Now means "at this moment" or "these days": I'm busy now. She lives in Paris now. Right now and just now add emphasis or mean "a moment ago".

Now as a discourse marker

At the start of a sentence, now introduces a new topic or gets attention: Now, let's look at the next question. Now then, what's the problem?

Now (that)

Now (that) means "because, at this point": Now that you're here, we can start.

Often

Often means "many times". It goes in mid position, or at the end with very or quite:

  • I often go swimming.
  • Do you come here often?
  • We meet quite often.

Comparatives: more often (common) or oftener (rare). Every so often means "sometimes".

Once

Once as an adverb

Once means "one time" or "at some time in the past": I've been there once. She once lived in Rome. Once upon a time begins fairy tales. At once means "immediately".

Once as a conjunction

It means "as soon as" or "after": Once you've finished, you can go. Use a present tense, not will, after once: Once you will finish.

Soon

Soon means "after a short time": I'll see you soon. She soon realised her mistake.

Soon, early or quickly?

  • soon = a short time from now / from then: They'll be here soon.
  • early = before the usual or planned time: I got up early.
  • quickly = at speed: She walked quickly.

Soon after, as soon as

Soon after the war, they moved to Canada. As soon as I get home, I'll call you. (present tense after as soon as for the future)

Still

Still as an adverb

Still shows that a situation continues and hasn't stopped: She's still asleep. Do you still live in Leeds? In negatives it goes before the auxiliary: He still hasn't called.

It can also mean "even so" (contrast): It was raining. Still, we had a good time. And with comparatives it means "even more": still better.

Still as an adjective

As an adjective it means "not moving" or "without bubbles": Stand still! still water.

Typical errors

  • I have still not finished is possible, but the usual form is I still haven't finished.
  • Still vs yet: still = continuing; yet = expected but not happened: It's still raining. / It hasn't stopped yet.

Then

Then has several meanings:

  • at that time: I was a student then.
  • next: We had dinner and then went to the cinema.
  • in addition: It's too expensive, and then there's the travel cost.
  • in that case: "I'm not hungry." "Then don't eat it." / If you're tired, then go to bed.

Usually

Usually means "in most cases" and goes in mid position, or at the front for emphasis: I usually have toast for breakfast. Usually, the shop opens at nine.

Typical errors

  • Use the present simple, not continuous, for habits: I'm usually going to work by bus → I usually go to work by bus.
  • For past habits use used to: I used to live there (not I usually lived for a finished habit you no longer have, though it's possible in narratives).

Eventually

Eventually means "in the end, after a long time or a lot of effort": After three hours we eventually found the hotel.

False friend

In many European languages a similar word means "possibly" or "perhaps". In English eventually never means "possibly". For that meaning use possibly or perhaps: We could possibly meet on Friday.

Adverbs as discourse markers (anyway, finally)

Some adverbs organise what we say and show how one part of a conversation or text relates to another.

Use Adverbs
ordering first(ly), secondly, finally, lastly
changing the topic anyway, by the way, incidentally
adding also, besides, moreover, furthermore
contrast however, still, nevertheless, on the other hand
summing up basically, in short, overall
  • Anyway, I must go now.
  • Finally, I'd like to thank everyone for coming.

Adverbs as short responses (definitely, certainly)

In conversation, single adverbs often work as complete replies.

Attitude and viewpoint

  • "Will you come?" "Definitely." / "Probably." / "Possibly."
  • "Was it expensive?" "Absolutely."

Replies to requests

  • "Could you help me?" "Certainly." / "Of course."

Intensifying adverbs with not

  • "Are you angry?" "Not really." / "Absolutely not." / "Definitely not."
