Adjectives

Adjectives give us more information about people, things, places and ideas. They can describe qualities (a tall tree), feelings (a nervous student), colour, size, age, nationality, material and much more.

Meanings of adjectives

  • a beautiful view (opinion)
  • a wooden box (material)
  • an Irish singer (origin)

Oppositeness

Many adjectives come in pairs of opposites (antonyms): hot / cold, easy / difficult, rich / poor. Some opposites are made with a prefix: happy / unhappy, possible / impossible.

Gradable and ungradable

Gradable adjectives describe qualities that can be stronger or weaker, so we can use them with very, a bit, extremely and in comparatives: very cold, colder. Ungradable adjectives describe an absolute or extreme quality: dead, married, freezing, enormous. We don't normally say very freezing; we use absolutely or completely instead.

  • It's very cold today.
  • It's absolutely freezing today.

Adjectives: forms

Identifying adjectives

Adjectives don't change for number or gender in English: a young man, two young women. Many have typical endings that help you recognise them.

Forming adjectives from other words

Suffix Examples
-able / -ible comfortable, reasonable, visible
-al national, musical, central
-ful / -less careful, useful / careless, homeless
-ic economic, historic, artistic
-ive active, attractive, expensive
-ous dangerous, famous, nervous
-y sunny, windy, salty
-ish childish, reddish, Spanish

Prefixes usually change the meaning, often to the opposite: unfair, incomplete, dishonest, irregular, illegal, overcrowded.

Adjectives with -ing and -ed (interesting, interested)

Adjectives ending in -ing describe the thing or person that causes a feeling. Adjectives ending in -ed describe the person who has the feeling.

  • The film was boring. (the film causes the feeling)
  • I was bored. (I have the feeling)
  • Not: I was boring during the film. (this means other people found you dull)

-ing versus -ed

She is interesting is a compliment about her. She is interested says she wants to know more. Mixing them up is one of the most common learner errors.

Adjectives: order

When several adjectives come before a noun, opinion adjectives usually come before fact adjectives: a lovely red dress, not a red lovely dress.

The usual order of fact adjectives is:

Type Examples
1 opinion lovely, strange, useful
2 size big, tiny, tall
3 physical quality thin, rough, smooth
4 shape round, square
5 age old, new, young
6 colour red, pale blue
7 origin French, Chinese
8 material wooden, plastic, silk
9 type electric, four-door
10 purpose cooking (pot), running (shoes)
  • a small round wooden table
  • a beautiful old Italian car

In real speech we rarely use more than two or three adjectives before a noun.

Adjectives joined by and

After a linking verb such as be, the last two adjectives are joined with and: The room was cold, dark and damp. Before a noun we use and when the adjectives are of the same type or describe different parts: a black and white photo.

Adjective phrases: functions

An adjective phrase is an adjective with words that modify or complete it: very tired, afraid of spiders, good enough to win.

Adjective phrases with nouns

They describe a noun, usually from before it (attributive): a really expensive watch.

Adjective phrases with verbs

After linking verbs (be, seem, look, feel, become, get, sound, taste) they describe the subject (predicative):

  • Brenda is happy.
  • You look tired.
  • The milk has gone sour.

Object complements

After some verbs, an adjective describes the object: The news made her angry. They painted the door green. I find this difficult.

Adjective phrases: position

Most adjectives can go before a noun or after a linking verb: a busy day / The day was busy. Some can only go in one place.

Adjectives used only before a noun

Group Examples
numbers, first, last the first time, the last bus
degree a complete idiot, utter nonsense, a total failure
time and order the former president, a future star
limiting the main reason, the only answer, the chief problem
nouns used as modifiers a tennis ball, a city centre

Adjectives used only after a noun

A few adjectives follow the noun, often in fixed phrases or with a different meaning: the people concerned, the president elect, the members present, something interesting.

Adjectives used only after a verb

Adjectives beginning with a- (afraid, alive, alone, asleep, awake, alike, aware) and some health adjectives (ill, well, unwell, fine) normally come after a verb.

  • The baby is asleep. Not: the asleep baby → the sleeping baby
  • My brother is ill. → before a noun: a sick child

Words that go before adjectives

Gradable adjectives take words like very, extremely, a bit, rather, quite, fairly, too, so. Extreme or absolute adjectives take absolutely, completely, totally, utterly. Really works with both: really cold, really freezing.

Different meanings before the noun and after the verb

  • the late Mr Jones (dead) / Mr Jones is late (not on time)
  • a concerned mother (worried) / the people concerned (involved)
  • the present situation (now) / the people present (there)

Special cases: quite, rather, as, so

With an article, quite and rather usually go before a/an: quite a cold day, rather a long walk (also a rather long walk). As and so go before the adjective and then the article: I've never seen so tall a man (formal), more often such a tall man.

Adjectives and adjective phrases: typical errors

  • Adjectives have no plural form: the reds cars → the red cars
  • Use an adjective, not an adverb, after linking verbs: She looks beautifully. → She looks beautiful.
  • Opinion before fact: a black nice jacket → a nice black jacket
  • -ing/-ed: I'm very interesting in history. → I'm very interested in history.
  • Words like alone, asleep, afraid don't go before a noun: an afraid dog → a frightened dog

Comparison: adjectives (bigger, biggest, more interesting)

We use comparative adjectives to compare two things and superlative adjectives to say one thing is at the top or bottom of a group.

  • My car is older than yours.
  • This is the oldest building in the town.

Forms

Adjective Comparative Superlative
one syllable: tall, cold taller, colder the tallest, the coldest
ends in -e: nice, late nicer the nicest
one vowel + one consonant: big, hot bigger, hotter the biggest
two syllables ending in -y: happy, easy happier the happiest
other two-syllable and longer: careful, expensive more careful the most expensive
irregular: good, bad, far better, worse, further/farther the best, the worst, the furthest

Some two-syllable adjectives can take either form: clever, narrow, quiet, simple, gentle → cleverer / more clever.

Using much, a lot, far with comparatives

To show a big or small difference, use much, a lot, far, way (informal), a bit, a little, slightly:

  • This flat is much bigger than the last one.
  • She's a bit taller than me.
  • Not: very bigger

Than, and -er and -er, the -er the -er

  • Trains are faster than buses.
  • It's getting colder and colder. / more and more expensive
  • The sooner we leave, the better. The more you practise, the easier it gets.

After than and as, informal English uses object pronouns (taller than me); formal English prefers subject + verb (taller than I am).

Less and not as … as

Less is the opposite of more: less expensive. With one-syllable adjectives, not as … as sounds more natural: not as tall as, rather than less tall.

Superlatives: the, prepositions and emphasis

We usually use the (or a possessive) with superlatives: the best, my best friend. After a superlative, use in for places and groups and of for periods and plural nouns:

  • the tallest building in the world. Not: of the world
  • the happiest day of my life
  • by far the best, easily the most popular, the very best
  • She was the first woman to win the prize. (to-infinitive after superlatives and ordinals)

Typical errors

  • more better, more easier → better, easier
  • the most cheapest → the cheapest
  • bigger as, bigger that → bigger than

Comparison: clauses (bigger than we had imagined)

A clause can follow than or as, and we often leave out repeated words:

  • The house was bigger than we had imagined.
  • He earns more than he did last year.
  • It's not as easy as it looks.

After so or such, use a that-clause for a result: It was so hot that we stayed inside. It was such a good film that I saw it twice.

Comparison: comparisons of equality (as tall as his father)

As + adjective/adverb + as says two things are equal: Tom is as tall as his father. The negative not as/so … as says they are not equal: It's not as cold as yesterday.

With nouns, use as much/many + noun + as or the same + noun + as:

  • I haven't got as much time as you.
  • She's the same age as my sister.

Add just, nearly, almost, twice, three times, half in front: just as good, twice as expensive, nowhere near as fast.

As … as

Not as … as

Not as … as is very common and sounds softer than a comparative: He's not as clever as he thinks (= less clever).

As … as + possibility

  • Come as soon as possible. / as quickly as you can
  • I'll help you as much as I can.

As much as, as many as

These can emphasise a large number or amount: Tickets cost as much as £300. There were as many as 50,000 people at the concert.

Fixed similes

English has many fixed as … as phrases: as cold as ice, as light as a feather, as busy as a bee, as old as the hills. Use them sparingly; they can sound like clichés in writing.
