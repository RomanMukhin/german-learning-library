Permit or permission?

Permission (uncountable) is the act of allowing: You need permission to film here. A permit /ˈpɜːmɪt/ (countable) is an official document: a work permit. Permit /pəˈmɪt/ is also a formal verb meaning allow.

Person, persons or people?

The normal plural of person is people: one person, two people. Persons is used only in very formal and legal language: Lift capacity: 8 persons. Peoples means nations or ethnic groups: the peoples of Africa.

Pick or pick up?

Pick = choose, or take fruit and flowers from a plant: pick a number, pick apples. Pick up = lift something, collect someone, or learn without studying: Pick up your coat. I'll pick you up at six. I picked up some Spanish.

Play or game?

A game is an activity with rules: a game of chess, computer games. A play is a piece of theatre: a play by Shakespeare. Play is also the verb: They play football. In sport, play (uncountable) means the action: Rain stopped play.

Politics, political, politician or policy?

  • politics (usually with a singular verb) = activities of government: She's interested in politics.
  • political = adjective: a political party
  • politician = a person in politics
  • policy = a plan of action of a government or organisation: the company's policy on holidays

Price or prize?

A price is the money you pay for something: What's the price of this? A prize is something you win: She won first prize.

Principal or principle?

Principal = main (adjective), or the head of a school or college (noun): the principal reason. Principle (noun only) = a basic rule or belief: It's against my principles.

Quiet or quite?

Quiet /ˈkwaɪət/ (two syllables) = not noisy: a quiet street. Quite /kwaɪt/ (one syllable) = fairly, or completely: It's quite good.

Raise or rise?

Raise has an object: you raise something: Raise your hand. They raised prices. Rise (rose, risen) has no object: something rises by itself: Prices rose. The sun rises at six. In British English, an increase in salary is a pay rise; in American English a raise.

Remember or remind?

You remember something yourself; someone or something reminds you.

  • I remember my first day at school.
  • Remind me to call her. Not: Remember me to call her
  • This song reminds me of summer.

Right or rightly?

Right as an adverb means "correctly" or "exactly": Did I spell it right? right in the middle. Rightly means "for a good reason, justifiably": She was rightly angry.

Rob or steal?

You steal a thing (from someone or somewhere); you rob a person or place (of something).

  • Someone stole my bike.
  • They robbed a bank. / I was robbed. Not: My bike was robbed.

Say or tell?

Tell needs a person as the object: She told me the news. Say doesn't take a person directly; use to: She said (to me) that she was tired.

  • He said hello. / He told a joke / a story / the truth / a lie.
  • Not: He said me

So that or in order that?

Both introduce purpose clauses. So that is more common; in order that is formal. Both are often followed by a modal verb:

  • I left early so that I could catch the bus.

Sometimes or sometime?

Sometimes = on some occasions: I sometimes work at home. Sometime = at an unknown or unspecified time: Let's meet sometime next week. Some time (two words) = a period of time: It took some time.

Sound or noise?

A sound is anything you hear: the sound of the sea. A noise is usually a loud or unpleasant sound: Stop making that noise!

Speak or talk?

Speak is more formal and is used for languages and one person addressing a group: Do you speak French? She spoke at the conference. Talk is more common for conversations: We talked for hours. Can I talk to you for a minute?

Such or so?

So goes before an adjective or adverb alone; such (a/an) goes before a noun (with or without an adjective).

  • The film was so good.
  • It was such a good film. / such nice people
  • Not: a so good film

There, their or they're?

  • there = in that place, or in there is / there are: Put it there. There are two cats.
  • their = belonging to them: their house
  • they're = they are: They're late.

Towards or toward?

They mean the same. Towards is more common in British English; toward in American English.

Wait or wait for?

Wait has no direct object; use wait for + noun/pronoun: I'm waiting for you. Not: I'm waiting you. Wait for someone to do something: We waited for the rain to stop. Await (formal) takes a direct object: We await your reply.

Wake, wake up or awaken?

Wake (up) is the usual verb, with or without an object: I woke up at seven. Wake me up at six. Awaken and awake are formal or literary.

Worth or worthwhile?

Worth is followed by a noun, an amount or an -ing form: The car is worth £5,000. The film is worth seeing. Worthwhile means "worth the time or effort" and usually stands alone or before a noun: a worthwhile project. It's worthwhile visiting the museum is also possible.
