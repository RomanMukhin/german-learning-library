Especially or specially?

Especially = particularly, above all: I love fruit, especially mangoes. Specially = for a particular purpose: This cake was made specially for you. In speech the two often overlap, but especially is the safe choice for "particularly".

Except or except for?

After all, every, no, nothing, everyone, both except and except for are possible: Everyone came except (for) Jo. At the start of a sentence use except for: Except for the weather, it was a great holiday. Except (not except for) is used before prepositions and clauses: It's the same except that it's red.

Expect, hope or wait?

  • expect = think something will happen: I expect she'll be late.
  • hope = want something to happen: I hope you feel better soon.
  • wait (for) = stay until something happens: I'm waiting for the bus.

Experience or experiment?

An experiment is a scientific test: a chemistry experiment. Experience (uncountable) is knowledge or skill gained by doing something: She has a lot of experience. An experience (countable) is an event that affects you: It was an amazing experience.

Fall or fall down?

Fall = move down suddenly, often from a height: The apple fell from the tree. Fall down / fall over = fall to the ground from standing: He slipped and fell down. Prices fall (not fall down).

Far or a long way?

Far is mainly used in questions and negatives: How far is it? It's not far. In affirmative sentences, a long way is more natural: It's a long way from here, rather than It's far from here (possible but less common). Too far and so far are fine in affirmative sentences.

Farther, farthest or further, furthest?

For physical distance, both are possible: the farther / further side of the lake. Only further means "more" or "additional": further information, until further notice. Further is more common in British English.

Fast, quick or quickly?

Fast is both an adjective and an adverb, used for speed of movement: a fast car, drive fast. Quick is an adjective, often for something that takes a short time: a quick shower. Quickly is the adverb: She finished quickly.

Fell or felt?

Fell is the past of fall: I fell off my bike. Felt is the past of feel: I felt tired.

Female or feminine; male or masculine?

Female and male refer to biological sex: a female doctor, male students. Feminine and masculine refer to qualities seen as typical of women or men, and to grammatical gender: a feminine voice, a masculine noun.

Finally, at last, lastly or in the end?

  • finally = after a long time, or to introduce the last point: We finally found it. / Finally, let me thank…
  • at last = after a long wait, with relief: At last you're here!
  • lastly = introducing the last item in a list: Lastly, check your spelling.
  • in the end = after many changes or problems: In the end we stayed at home.

First, firstly or at first?

First and firstly introduce the first point in a list (first is more common). At first means "at the beginning", in contrast with later: At first I hated the job, but now I love it.

Fit or suit?

Fit = be the right size and shape: These shoes don't fit me. Suit = look good on someone, or be convenient: That colour suits you. Does Friday suit you?

Following or the following?

The following introduces what comes next in a text: The following people should attend: … Following without the is a preposition meaning "after": Following the meeting, there will be lunch.

For or since?

Both are used with the present perfect. For + a period of time; since + a point in time.

  • I've lived here for ten years.
  • I've lived here since 2015.

Forget or leave?

To say where you left something, use leave, not forget: I left my keys at home. Not: I forgot my keys at home. You can say I forgot my keys without a place.

Full or filled?

Full (of) describes a state: The glass is full. The room was full of people. Filled (with) suggests that someone filled it: The glass was filled with water.

Fun or funny?

Fun means enjoyable; funny means it makes you laugh (or is strange).

  • The party was great fun. Not: very funny (unless it made you laugh)
  • That joke is so funny.
  • There's a funny smell in here. (strange)

Get or go?

Go is about movement towards a place; get to focuses on arriving: How do I get to the station? What time did you get home? (no to before home) Get also means "become": It's getting dark.

Grateful or thankful?

Grateful is used for thanking people for what they did: I'm very grateful to you for your help. Thankful is used when you're glad something good happened or something bad didn't: I'm thankful that nobody was hurt.

Hear or listen (to)?

Hear = sounds reach your ears, whether you want them to or not. Listen (to) = pay attention to sounds on purpose.

  • I heard a strange noise.
  • I like listening to music. Not: listening music
