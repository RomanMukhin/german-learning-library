Pronouns

Pronouns replace nouns or noun phrases so we don't have to repeat them: Anna called. She wanted to talk to you. Main types:

Type Examples
personal I, me, you, she, her, it, we, us, they, them
possessive mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs
reflexive myself, yourself, themselves
indefinite someone, anything, nobody, everywhere
demonstrative this, that, these, those
relative who, whom, whose, which, that
interrogative who, what, which, whose
reciprocal each other, one another

Many determiners can also be pronouns: some, any, both, all, each, neither, many.

Each other, one another

Each other and one another show that two or more people do the same thing to the other(s): Tom and Sue love each other. (= Tom loves Sue and Sue loves Tom). One another is a little more formal. Possessive: each other's houses.

Typical errors

  • Don't use a reflexive: They looked at themselves (= each in a mirror) ≠ They looked at each other.
  • Some verbs don't need each other: They met in 2010, We kissed.

Everyone, everybody, everything, everywhere

Everyone and everybody mean "all people" and are the same (everybody is a little more informal). They take a singular verb, but are referred back to with they/their:

  • Everyone has finished, haven't they?
  • Everything is ready. / I looked everywhere.

Typical error

  • Everyone (all people) ≠ every one (each single item): every one of the cakes.

It

Empty or dummy it

It can be a subject with no real meaning, for weather, time, distance and situations:

  • It's raining. / It's ten o'clock. / It's 5 km to the station. / It's nice here.

Anticipatory it

When the real subject is a long clause, we often put it first and the clause at the end:

  • It's important to drink water. (more natural than To drink water is important.)
  • It was clear that she was tired.
  • I find it amazing that he passed. (anticipatory object)

Cleft sentences and passives

It + be + focus + who/that emphasises one part: It was my sister who told me. With reporting verbs in the passive: It is said that the house is haunted.

Gender

English nouns have no grammatical gender. We use he/she for people and sometimes pets, it for things and most animals. When the sex is unknown or not important, use they/them/their (singular they): If a student is ill, they should call the office. Writers sometimes use he or she, but they is now standard. Some people also choose they as their personal pronoun.

No one, nobody, nothing, nowhere

No one or nobody?

Both mean "not anybody" and are the same; nobody is a little more informal. No one is usually written as two words (sometimes no-one in British English). They take a singular verb: Nobody knows.

Nobody or not … anybody?

Nobody came = There wasn't anybody. Don't use two negatives: I didn't see nobody → I didn't see anybody / I saw nobody. At the start of a sentence only the no- form is possible: Nothing happened. Not: Anything didn't happen.

One

One can be a number (one apple), a pronoun replacing a countable noun, or a general pronoun meaning "people in general".

  • I don't like this shirt. Have you got a blue one? / I prefer the ones in the window.
  • Not with uncountable nouns: I like white wine, but not red one → …but not red.

Don't use one directly after a possessive or a without an adjective: That's my car (not my one in formal English), I need a pen. Have you got one?

One and one's

As a general pronoun, one means "any person, including me". It is very formal: One should always be polite. The possessive is one's: to do one's best. The reflexive is oneself. In everyday English we use you instead.

Pronouns: personal (I, me, you, him, it, they)

Subject I you he she it we they
Object me you him her it us them

Subject pronouns go before the verb; object pronouns go after verbs and prepositions: She called me. Come with us.

  • After be, than and as, informal English uses object pronouns: It's me. She's taller than me.
  • In pairs, put yourself last: My friend and I went. (subject) / She invited my friend and me. (object). Not: Me and my friend went in formal English.
  • You is both singular and plural. They can refer to one person of unknown gender.
  • We can include or exclude the listener: Shall we go? (you and me) / We're open on Sundays. (our company)

Pronouns: possessive (my, mine, your, yours)

Determiner my your his her its our their
Pronoun mine yours his hers — ours theirs

Possessive determiners go before a noun; possessive pronouns stand alone:

  • This is my bag. / This bag is mine.

Typical errors

  • No apostrophe: your's, her's, their's, it's (possessive) → yours, hers, theirs, its
  • Possessives replace articles: the my car → my car; a friend of mine, not a my friend
  • Use possessives with body parts: She broke her arm, not the arm.

Pronouns: reflexive (myself, themselves)

myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves

Same subject and object

  • I cut myself. / Did you enjoy yourselves? / Behave yourself!

Many verbs are not reflexive in English, unlike in other languages: wash, dress, shave, relax, feel, meet, concentrate usually have no reflexive: I got up and washed, not washed myself (possible for emphasis).

For emphasis

  • The manager herself came to apologise. / I made it myself.

By + reflexive = alone

  • She lives by herself. / I did it (all) by myself. (without help)

Politeness

Some speakers use myself instead of I/me to sound polite (Contact John or myself), but many consider this incorrect: use me.

Pronouns: indefinite (-body, -one, -thing, -where)

some- any- no- every-
people someone / somebody anyone / anybody no one / nobody everyone / everybody
things something anything nothing everything
places somewhere anywhere nowhere everywhere

Some- forms are typical in affirmatives and offers; any- forms in questions and negatives; no- forms are negative already. They take singular verbs.

Somewhere nice, nothing much

Adjectives and else come after these pronouns: something new, somewhere warm, nothing special, someone else. Not: new something.

Pronouns: one, you, we, they

To talk about people in general:

  • you (most common, informal): You can't smoke here.
  • one (very formal): One must be careful.
  • we (people including the speaker): We all need friends.
  • they (other people, authorities): They're building a new road. They say it will rain.

Relative pronouns

Relative pronouns introduce relative clauses, which give more information about a noun.

Pronoun Refers to Example
who people the woman who lives next door
whom people, as object (formal) the man whom I met
whose possession the girl whose father is a pilot
which things, animals, whole sentences the car which I bought
that people or things (defining clauses only) the book that I read
when / where / why time / place / reason the day when we met, the town where I grew up, the reason why

Subjects and objects; leaving out the pronoun

When the relative pronoun is the object of a defining clause, we can leave it out: the book (that) I read. When it is the subject, we can't: the man who called (not the man called in that meaning).

With prepositions

Formal: the person to whom I spoke. Informal: the person (who/that) I spoke to. After a preposition use whom or which, never who or that.

That after superlatives; which for whole sentences

It's the best film that I've ever seen. (that is preferred after superlatives, all, everything, nothing) / He passed the exam, which surprised everyone. (refers to the whole clause; never that here)

Typical errors

  • the man which → the man who
  • the book that I read it → the book that I read (no extra pronoun)
  • My brother, that lives in Rome, … → My brother, who lives in Rome, (no that in non-defining clauses)

Questions: interrogative pronouns (what, who)

Who, whom, what, which and whose can be pronouns that ask questions:

  • Who called? (subject) / Who did you call? (object)
  • What happened? / Which do you prefer?

Who or whom?

Whom is the formal object form, used mainly after prepositions: To whom did you give it? In everyday English we say Who did you give it to?

What or which?

What asks about an open range of possibilities; which asks about a limited, known set: What would you like to drink? / Which do you want, tea or coffee?

Someone, somebody, something, somewhere

Someone and somebody

Both mean "a person, not named"; somebody is a bit more informal. They take a singular verb but they as the pronoun: Someone left their umbrella.

Something and anything

Use something in affirmatives, offers and requests, even in questions when you expect "yes": Would you like something to drink? Use anything in negatives and open questions: I didn't buy anything. Is there anything I can do?

That

Determiner and pronoun

That (plural those) points to something further away: That house is old. Is that yours?

Relative pronoun

In defining relative clauses: The film that we saw was great.

That-clauses

That introduces reported statements and clauses after many verbs and adjectives, and it is often left out in speech: She said (that) she was tired. I'm sure (that) he'll come.

Other uses

  • That's great / That's right! (that's + adjective in replies)
  • intensifier = so (informal): It wasn't that bad. I'm not that hungry.
