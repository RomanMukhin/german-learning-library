Nouns

Types of nouns

  • common nouns — general things: city, dog, happiness
  • proper nouns — names, with a capital letter: Paris, Maria, Monday, the Thames
  • concrete nouns — things you can see or touch: table, rain
  • abstract nouns — ideas, feelings, qualities: love, freedom, idea
  • collective nouns — groups: team, family, audience

Nouns for people often show the role: teacher, driver, employee, cyclist. Nouns for animals and things use it (pets may be he/she).

Identifying nouns

Nouns can follow a determiner (the book), be plural (books), take an adjective before them (a good book) and be the subject or object of a verb.

Gerunds

The -ing form of a verb can work as a noun: Swimming is good exercise. I enjoy reading.

Nouns: form

Most nouns add -s for the plural. Spelling rules:

Ending Rule Example
-s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x, -z + es buses, classes, boxes, watches
consonant + y y → ies city → cities
vowel + y + s days, keys
-f / -fe (many) → ves leaf → leaves, knife → knives (but roofs, chefs)
consonant + o (some) + es potatoes, tomatoes, heroes (but photos, pianos)

Irregular plurals: man → men, woman → women, child → children, person → people, foot → feet, tooth → teeth, mouse → mice. Some nouns don't change: sheep, fish, deer, aircraft, series, species.

Nouns and prepositions

Many nouns are followed by a particular preposition:

  • a reason for, the cause of, an increase in, a solution to, an interest in, damage to, a demand for, the difference between, a relationship with

Typical errors

  • the reason of → the reason for
  • a solution of the problem → a solution to the problem
  • an increase of prices → an increase in prices

Nouns: compound nouns

A compound noun is made of two or more words that work as one noun.

  • noun + noun: bus stop, toothpaste, credit card
  • adjective + noun: blackboard, greenhouse, software
  • verb + noun or -ing + noun: swimming pool, washing machine, runway

Writing compound nouns

They can be one word (bedroom), two words (post office) or hyphenated (mother-in-law). Check a dictionary; there is no fixed rule.

Plurals and stress

Usually the last word becomes plural: bus stops, toothbrushes. With -in-law and passer-by, the first: mothers-in-law. The main stress usually falls on the first part: /ˈbʌs stɒp/, GREENhouse (a building) vs green HOUSE (a house painted green).

Nouns: countable and uncountable

Countable nouns

They have singular and plural forms and can follow a/an or a number: a chair, two chairs. A singular countable noun always needs a determiner: I have car. → I have a car.

Uncountable nouns

They have no plural and don't take a/an: water, money, advice, information, music, luggage. They take a singular verb: The news is good.

Quantity expressions

To count them, use a piece word: a piece of advice, a bit of luck, a slice of bread, a bottle of water, an item of news.

Determiners

Countable plural Uncountable
many, (a) few, fewer, several much, (a) little, less
some, any, a lot of, plenty of, no, enough (same)

Different meanings

Some nouns are countable with one meaning and uncountable with another:

  • a glass (container) / glass (material)
  • a paper (newspaper) / paper (material)
  • a chicken (animal) / chicken (meat)
  • a room / There's no room (space)
  • an experience (event) / experience (skill)

Uncountable nouns used countably

In informal speech, drinks and food can be counted as portions: Two coffees, please. Abstract nouns can become countable when they mean a type or instance: a fear of spiders, a good education.

Nouns: forming nouns from other words

Many nouns are formed with suffixes:

From Suffix Examples
verbs -ment, -tion/-sion, -ance/-ence, -al, -er/-or development, decision, performance, arrival, driver, actor
adjectives -ness, -ity, -ence, -dom happiness, ability, confidence, freedom
nouns -ship, -hood, -ism, -ist friendship, childhood, tourism, artist

Some verbs and nouns have the same form: a walk, a call, a change, a visit.

Nouns: singular and plural

Nouns only in the singular

Some nouns ending in -s take a singular verb: news, mathematics, physics, athletics, measles. The news is on at six.

Nouns only in the plural

  • tools and instruments with two parts: scissors, glasses, binoculars, scales
  • clothes with two legs: trousers, jeans, shorts, pyjamas, tights
  • others: clothes, goods, outskirts, stairs, thanks, congratulations, belongings, surroundings

To count them, use a pair of: a pair of jeans, two pairs of scissors.

Police and people are plural without -s: The police are coming.

Collective nouns

Group words like team, family, government, staff, class, company, audience can take a singular or plural verb in British English: The team is / are playing well. Use plural when you think of the members, singular when you think of the group as one unit. American English usually uses singular.

Accommodation

Accommodation (a place to live or stay) is uncountable in British English: We need to find accommodation. Not: an accommodation, accommodations (American English often uses accommodations).

Spelling: double c and double m: accommodation.

Equipment

Equipment is uncountable: The lab has modern equipment. Not: equipments. Count it with a piece of equipment.

Furniture

Furniture is uncountable: We bought new furniture. Not: furnitures, a furniture. Use a piece of furniture or name the item (a chair, a table).

Information

Information is uncountable: Can you give me some information about the course? Not: informations, an information. Use a piece of information. It is followed by about or on.

Luck and lucky

Luck is an uncountable noun: Good luck! It was bad luck. Not: a good luck. Lucky is the adjective: You're lucky. It's lucky that you came. Be in luck = be lucky at a particular moment.

News

News is uncountable and takes a singular verb, even though it ends in -s: The news is good. Not: a news, The news are. One item is a piece of news or some news.

Progress

Progress is uncountable: She's making good progress. Not: a progress, progresses. The verb is make progress, not do progress.

Weather

Weather is uncountable: What lovely weather! Not: What a lovely weather! Use the weather for the weather at a particular time: How's the weather?. Don't confuse with whether (= if).
