Past simple (I worked)

Form

Regular Irregular
+ I / she / they worked I went
− I didn't work I didn't go
? Did you work? Did you go?

After did / didn't, use the base form: Did you went? → Did you go? Be has its own forms: was / were, wasn't / weren't.

Pronunciation of -ed

  • /t/ after voiceless sounds: worked, stopped, washed
  • /d/ after voiced sounds: played, lived, opened
  • /ɪd/ after t and d: wanted, needed

Irregular verbs

Many common verbs are irregular: go – went, have – had, see – saw, buy – bought. See the Table of irregular verbs.

Uses

  • finished actions at a definite time in the past: I saw her yesterday. She left in 2019.
  • single events, habits and states in the past: We walked to school every day. He lived in Rome for ten years (and doesn't now).
  • with no time word when the time is clear: Who painted this?
  • a sequence of events, in order: She opened the door, looked around and went in.

Past continuous (I was working)

Form

was / were + -ing: I was working. They weren't listening. Were you sleeping?

Uses

  • an action in progress at a particular past time: At 8 p.m. I was having dinner.
  • background to a story: The sun was shining and birds were singing.
  • an action interrupted by a shorter one (past simple): I was cooking when the phone rang.
  • two actions in progress at the same time: While I was reading, he was watching TV.
  • repeated or annoying actions (with always): She was always complaining.

State verbs (know, like, want) are not normally used in the continuous: I was knowing → I knew.

Past continuous or past simple?

The choice depends on what you want to emphasise:

  • past continuous = the action was in progress, unfinished, background
  • past simple = the action is complete, a main event
  • I was writing an email. (I may not have finished) / I wrote an email. (finished)
  • When she arrived, we were having lunch. (lunch had started) / When she arrived, we had lunch. (lunch started after she arrived)

Past simple or present perfect?

Past simple Present perfect
finished time: yesterday, last week, in 2010, ago, when…? time up to now: ever, never, already, yet, just, so far, since, for (still true)
I lost my keys yesterday. I've lost my keys. (I still can't find them)
She worked here for 5 years. (she doesn't now) She's worked here for 5 years. (she still does)

Don't use the present perfect with finished time words: I have seen him yesterday. → I saw him yesterday. When did you arrive? (not When have you arrived?)

Used to

Meaning and form

Used to + base form describes past habits or states that are no longer true: I used to play tennis. (but I don't now) There used to be a cinema here.

Negatives, questions, emphasis and tags

  • negative: I didn't use to like coffee. (formal: I used not to)
  • question: Did you use to live here?
  • emphatic: I did use to smoke, but I stopped.
  • tag: You used to live in Paris, didn't you?

Used to or would?

Would can also describe repeated past actions, especially in stories (Every summer we would go to the sea), but not states: I would have long hair → I used to have long hair.

Used to or be used to?

Be / get used to + noun or -ing means "be / become accustomed to": I'm used to getting up early. She got used to the cold. Different meaning from used to + base form.

Past perfect simple (I had worked)

Form

had + past participle: I had finished. She hadn't seen it. Had they left? Short form: I'd, she'd.

Uses

  • an action before another past action: When I arrived, the film had already started. She told me she had lost her job.
  • time up to a point in the past: By 2010 he had lived there for 20 years.
  • reported speech: He said he had seen it.
  • changed states and unfulfilled hopes: I had hoped to see you.
  • in third conditionals: If I had known, I would have helped.

When the order is clear from words like after or before, the past simple is often enough: After she left / had left, I went to bed.

Past perfect continuous (I had been working)

Form

had been + -ing: She had been waiting for an hour.

Uses

It describes a continuing activity up to a past point, often with a visible result or with a focus on duration: His eyes were red. He had been crying. We had been driving for six hours when the car broke down.

Past perfect simple or past perfect continuous?

Use the simple for completed actions and for how many / how much: She had written three letters. Use the continuous for the activity in progress and its duration: She had been writing letters all morning. State verbs take the simple form: I had known him for years (not had been knowing).

Past perfect simple or past simple?

Use the past simple for events in the order they happened. Use the past perfect to go back to an earlier time:

  • The train left when I arrived. (I saw it leave)
  • The train had left when I arrived. (it was gone before I got there)

Past verb forms referring to the present

Being polite or less direct

Past forms make requests and questions softer: I wanted to ask you something. I was wondering if you could help. Did you want a drink?

Things in the past that are still true

What was your name again? (= What is your name? I've forgotten.)

Unreal present situations

After if, wish, would rather, it's time, past forms refer to present or unreal situations: If I had money, I'd travel. I wish I knew. It's time we left.

Past: typical errors

  • Base form after did: I didn't went → I didn't go
  • Finished time + present perfect: I have visited Rome last year → I visited Rome last year
  • State verbs in the continuous: I was having a car → I had a car
  • Used to for present habits: I use to get up at 7 → I usually get up at 7
  • Irregular forms: buyed, catched, thinked → bought, caught, thought
