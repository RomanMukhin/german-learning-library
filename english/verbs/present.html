Present continuous (I am working)

Form

am / is / are + -ing: I'm working. She isn't listening. Are you coming?

-ing spelling

  • most verbs + -ing: play → playing
  • final silent -e dropped: make → making, write → writing (but see → seeing)
  • double the final consonant after a short stressed vowel: run → running, begin → beginning
  • -ie → -ying: lie → lying, die → dying

Uses

  • actions happening now: Look! It's snowing.
  • temporary situations: I'm living with my parents at the moment.
  • repeated temporary events: I'm going to the gym a lot these days.
  • changing situations: The climate is getting warmer.
  • annoying habits with always: You're always losing things!
  • future arrangements: I'm meeting Sam tomorrow.

Present perfect continuous (I have been working)

Form

have / has been + -ing: I've been waiting for an hour. How long have you been learning English?

Uses

  • a recent activity with a present result: I'm tired because I've been running. It's been raining (the streets are wet).
  • an activity that started in the past and continues now, often with for, since, how long: She's been working here since May.
  • repeated continuing activities: I've been calling you all day!

Present perfect simple (I have worked)

Form

have / has + past participle: I've finished. He hasn't called. Have you eaten?

Uses

  • experiences up to now (time not stated): Have you ever been to India? I've never seen snow.
  • recent completed events, often with just: I've just had lunch.
  • past events with present results: I've lost my phone. (I don't have it now)
  • unfinished time periods: I've drunk three coffees today.
  • situations continuing to now, with for and since: We've known each other for ten years / since 2015.

Yet, already, still

  • yet in questions and negatives, at the end: Have you finished yet? I haven't finished yet.
  • already (sooner than expected): I've already done it.
  • still + negative: She still hasn't replied.

Introducing news, then past simple for details

I've been to Paris. I went there last summer and stayed for a week.

American English

American English often uses the past simple where British English uses the present perfect: Did you eat yet? (US) / Have you eaten yet? (Br)

Present perfect simple or present perfect continuous?

Completed or continuing

  • simple = the result, how many, how much, completed: I've read three books this month.
  • continuous = the activity, duration, possibly unfinished: I've been reading all afternoon.
  • I've painted the kitchen. (finished) / I've been painting the kitchen. (maybe not finished; that's why I'm dirty)

Mental process and sense verbs

State verbs like know, believe, understand, own, see, hear use the simple form: I've known her for years. Not: I've been knowing.

Present perfect: typical errors

  • with finished past time: I've seen it last week. → I saw it last week.
  • present simple for duration up to now: I live here since 2010. → I've lived here since 2010.
  • How long are you here? → How long have you been here?
  • since + period: since two years → for two years

Present simple (I work)

Form
+ I / you / we / they work; he / she / it works
− I don't work; she doesn't work
? Do you work? Does she work?

Spelling of he / she / it forms

  • -es after s, sh, ch, x, o: watches, goes, does, fixes
  • consonant + y → -ies: study → studies (but play → plays)
  • have → has

Uses

  • general truths and facts: Water boils at 100 °C.
  • habits and routines: I get up at seven. She plays tennis on Fridays.
  • permanent situations: They live in Leeds.
  • instructions and directions: You turn left at the bank.
  • stories, jokes and commentaries: So a man walks into a bar…
  • immediate reactions and speech acts: I promise. I agree. I apologise.
  • timetables and fixed plans: The train leaves at 9.15.
  • future after when, if, as soon as, before, after, until: I'll call you when I get home.

Present simple or present continuous?

Permanent and temporary

  • I work in a bank. (permanent) / I'm working in a bank this summer. (temporary)
  • What do you do? (job) / What are you doing? (now)

Verbs not often used in the continuous

State verbs describe states, not actions: thinking and knowing (know, believe, understand, remember, mean), feelings (like, love, hate, want, prefer, need), senses (see, hear, smell, taste), possession (have, own, belong), and seem, cost, contain, depend.

  • I know the answer. Not: I'm knowing

Some have an action meaning too: I think it's good (opinion) / I'm thinking about it (process). She has a car / She's having lunch. This soup tastes good / I'm tasting the soup.

Be in the continuous

Be in the continuous describes temporary behaviour: You're being very rude today. (normally you aren't)

Present: typical errors

  • missing -s: She work here. → She works here.
  • missing do / does: Where you live? → Where do you live?
  • -s after does: Does she works? → Does she work?
  • continuous with state verbs: I'm wanting a coffee. → I want a coffee.
  • present simple for things happening now: Look, it rains! → Look, it's raining!

Present verb forms referring to the past

In informal storytelling and jokes, the present simple and continuous make past events vivid: So I'm walking down the street and this man comes up to me and says… Newspaper headlines also use the present simple for past events: PM RESIGNS. Verbs of communication often use the present for past messages: Sarah says you're moving. I hear you got the job.
