Collocation

Collocations are words that naturally go together. Native speakers say heavy rain (not strong rain), make a mistake (not do a mistake), fast food (not quick food).

Strong collocations

Strong collocations are fixed or almost fixed: pitch dark, bitterly cold, commit a crime, a burning ambition, pay attention, take a photo.

Weak collocations

Weak collocations combine freely with many words: a good / nice / great / long day, a big / small house.

Common patterns: adjective + noun (strong coffee, high price), verb + noun (make a decision, catch a cold), adverb + adjective (deeply sorry, fully aware), verb + adverb (whisper softly).

Learn word partners

When you learn a new noun, note the verbs and adjectives that go with it: a decision → make / take (Br) / reach a decision, a difficult / final decision.

According to

According to + a person or source reports what they say: According to the forecast, it will rain. According to Anna, the meeting is cancelled. It also means "following": Everything went according to plan.

Typical error

  • Don't use it for your own opinion: According to me → In my opinion / I think.

Actual and actually

Actual

Actual = real, true, exact: What was the actual cost? the actual words. It does not mean "current". For that, use current or present.

Actually

Actually = in fact, really. It is used to correct, contrast, add surprising information or soften:

  • "You're Spanish?" "Actually, I'm Portuguese." (correcting politely)
  • I thought it would be boring, but it was actually fun. (contrast)

Actual ≠ current

In many languages, the word that looks like actual means "current, now". In English, actually never means "at the moment". Use currently or at the moment.

Approximations (around four o'clock)

  • before a number: about / around / roughly / approximately 50, nearly / almost 50 (less than), over / more than 50
  • number + -odd: forty-odd people (informal)
  • number + noun + or so / or something / or thereabouts (formal): ten minutes or so
  • more or less + number: It's more or less 20 km.
  • or between numbers: two or three days
  • -ish (informal speech): at sixish, fortyish

At all

At all strengthens negatives and questions: I don't like it at all. Do you know him at all? If you need anything at all, ask. Not at all is a polite reply to thank you or Do you mind…?: "Do you mind if I sit here?" "Not at all."

At all and politeness

In questions, at all makes requests less direct: Is there any chance at all that you could help?

Else

Else with someone, anybody, nobody, etc.

Else means "other, different, more" and comes after indefinite pronouns and adverbs: someone else, anything else, nowhere else, everybody else. Possessive: someone else's coat.

Else with who, what, where

Who else was there? What else do you need? Where else can we go?

Or else

Or else = otherwise, often as a warning: Hurry up, or else we'll miss the train. Do it now, or else! (threat)

Hear that, see that

Hear that and see that + clause report information: I hear (that) you're getting married. I see (that) prices have gone up again. This use of the present simple means "I have heard / read". Compare perception: I heard her sing (whole action) / singing (part).

However, whatever, whichever, whenever, wherever, whoever

It doesn't matter how, what, when…

These words mean "it doesn't matter which / who / what": Whatever you do, don't tell her. Sit wherever you like. However hard I try, I can't do it. I'll call whenever I can. Whoever wins, it'll be a good match.

Emphasising questions

They show surprise or annoyance: Whatever are you doing? However did you manage that? Whoever told you that?

Being vague

Whatever alone is informal and can sound rude: "Do you want tea or coffee?" "Whatever."

Whatsoever

Whatsoever emphasises a negative: I have no idea whatsoever.

It's time

It's time to + verb: It's time to go. It's time for + noun / person + to: It's time for lunch. It's time for us to leave.

It's (high / about) time + subject + past simple means something should already have happened: It's time you went to bed. It's high time they fixed the road. (present meaning, past form)

May as well and might as well

Might / may as well + base verb suggests doing something because there is no better alternative or no reason not to: The shop's closed, so we might as well go home. We may as well wait. Might as well is more common.

More or less

More or less means "approximately" or "almost": I've more or less finished. It costs more or less £50. The two are more or less the same.

Of course

Of course means "certainly, naturally". It is used to give permission, agree, or show something is obvious: "Can I borrow your pen?" "Of course." Of course you can come.

Be careful: as an answer to a factual question, of course can sound like "That's obvious, why do you ask?": "Do you speak English?" "Yes, I do." is safer than Of course. Of course not = certainly not.

Point of view

Point of view means a way of considering something, or an opinion: from my point of view, from an economic point of view. In my point of view is incorrect: say in my view, in my opinion or from my point of view.
