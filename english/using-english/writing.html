Apostrophe (')

Contractions

An apostrophe shows that letters are missing: I'm (I am), don't (do not), it's (it is / has), we've, they'll.

Possession

  • singular noun + 's: the dog's bowl, James's car / James' car
  • plural ending in -s + ': the girls' room
  • irregular plural + 's: the children's toys, men's clothes

Time

a week's holiday, two weeks' notice, today's news, in a year's time

Typical error

  • Don't use an apostrophe for plurals: apple's £1 → apples £1; the 1990's (accepted but 1990s preferred)
  • its (possessive) has no apostrophe: The company and its staff

Apposition

Apposition means two noun phrases next to each other refer to the same person or thing: Ms Lee, our manager, will speak first.

Types of apposition

  • non-restrictive (extra information, commas): Rome, the capital of Italy, is beautiful.
  • restrictive (identifies, no commas): the poet Byron, my friend Sarah
  • with namely, that is (i.e.), for example (e.g.): two cities, namely Paris and Lyon

Contractions

With pronouns

I'm, you're, he's, she's, it's, we're, they're; I've, you've; I'll, you'll; I'd, you'd (would / had)

With auxiliaries and not

isn't, aren't, wasn't, weren't, don't, doesn't, didn't, haven't, hasn't, hadn't, won't (will not), can't, couldn't, shouldn't, wouldn't, mustn't /ˈmʌsnt/, needn't. Note: I'm not (not I amn't), aren't I?

Other contractions

let's (let us), there's, here's, what's, who's, where's, how's, informal gonna (going to), wanna (want to), y'all (US).

Contractions are normal in speech and informal writing; avoid them in formal writing.

Contrasts

On the one hand … on the other hand

Used to compare two sides of an argument: On the one hand, the job pays well. On the other hand, the hours are long.

On the contrary

On the contrary rejects what was said and says the opposite is true: "Was it boring?" "On the contrary, it was fascinating." Not for a simple contrast between two facts.

In comparison / by comparison; in contrast / by contrast

Our town is small. In comparison, London is enormous. Sales rose in the north. By contrast, they fell in the south. In contrast to / with + noun: In contrast to her sister, she is very shy.

Detached impersonal style

Formal writing (reports, academic texts, instructions) often avoids a personal tone by using:

  • few personal pronouns: It is recommended that… instead of I recommend…
  • noun phrases: the introduction of new rules instead of when they introduced new rules
  • the passive voice: The samples were analysed. It is believed that…

Internet discourse and text messages

Emails

Emails range from formal (Dear Mr Patel, … Kind regards) to very informal (Hi Sam, … Cheers). Neutral openings and closings: Hello / Hi + name, Best wishes, Thanks. Keep paragraphs short and subject lines clear.

Text messages and chat

Texts use abbreviations and ellipsis: lol, btw (by the way), thx, pls, u (you), gr8 (great), idk (I don't know), omg, emojis. Subjects and articles are often left out: Running late, there in 10. Don't use this style in formal writing.

It, this and that in paragraphs

These words refer back to earlier ideas and connect sentences:

  • it continues the same topic: The company opened in 2010. It now has 500 staff.
  • this refers to a new idea just mentioned and focuses on it: Prices rose by 20%. This caused protests.
  • that creates distance or contrast, or refers to a previous speaker's idea: "Let's cancel." "That's a good idea."

This + noun is very clear in writing: This decision caused problems.

Paragraphs

A paragraph is a group of sentences about one main idea. Typically it starts with a topic sentence that states the idea, continues with supporting sentences (examples, reasons, details), and may end with a concluding or linking sentence. Start a new paragraph for each new point, a new speaker in dialogue, or a change of time or place. In print, paragraphs are shown by indentation or a blank line.

Punctuation

Capital letters and full stops

Capitals start sentences and are used for names, days, months, nationalities, languages and I: On Monday, I met a French student called Marie. A full stop (Br) / period (US) ends a statement and follows some abbreviations (etc., e.g.).

Question marks and exclamation marks

? ends direct questions (not indirect: I asked where she was.). ! shows strong feeling or emphasis; use it rarely in formal writing.

Commas

Commas separate items in lists (eggs, milk and bread; the "Oxford comma" before and is optional in British English), and introductory phrases (However, …, In 2010, …).

  • separating clauses: use a comma before but, so, or joining two clauses, and after a fronted subordinate clause: If it rains, we'll stay in. Not before a that-clause: She said, that.
  • relative clauses: commas around non-defining clauses only: My brother, who lives in Rome, is a chef.
  • speech: "Wait," she said.

Colons and semi-colons

A colon (:) introduces a list, explanation or quote: You need three things: a pen, paper and time. A semi-colon (;) joins two closely related independent clauses, or separates long list items: It was late; we went home.

Quotation marks

British English often uses single marks ('…'), American double ("…"). They show direct speech, quotations and titles of short works.

Dashes, hyphens and brackets

A dash (–) adds a comment or a dramatic pause (informal): He was late – again. A hyphen (-) joins words: well-known, twenty-one. Brackets (…) add extra information.

Speech into writing

When we turn speech into writing, we usually: remove hesitations and fillers (er, you know, like), complete ellipted sentences, replace informal words with neutral ones, add clear linking words, and organise ideas into paragraphs. Direct speech is shown with quotation marks and reporting verbs; reported speech uses indirect forms.

Spelling

Prefixes

Adding a prefix doesn't change the spelling of the root: un + necessary = unnecessary, dis + appear = disappear, mis + spell = misspell. Il- goes before l (illegal), im- before m, p, b (impossible, immature), ir- before r (irregular).

Plurals

-s normally, -es after s, sh, ch, x, z (boxes), consonant + y → -ies (cities), many -f(e) → -ves (knives).

Doubling consonants

Double a final single consonant after a single stressed vowel before -ed, -ing, -er, -est: stop → stopped, big → bigger, begin → beginning (stress on second syllable). Not when unstressed: visit → visited, open → opening. British English also doubles l: travel → travelled (US traveled).

Dropping and adding letters

  • final silent -e is dropped before a vowel suffix: make → making, hope → hoped (but kept before a consonant: hopeful, and in agreeing, seeing)
  • adjectives ending in -ic take -ally: basically, dramatically (exception: publicly)
  • consonant + -y → -i-: happy → happiness, carry → carried (but carrying)

ie or ei?

The rule "i before e except after c" works for the /iː/ sound: believe, piece, field but receive, ceiling. Exceptions: seize, weird, either, neither, protein.

Verb forms

-ed: played, stopped, tried, agreed. -ing: playing, stopping, trying, lying (lie → lying, die → dying).

British and American spelling

-our / -or (colour / color), -re / -er (centre / center), -ise / -ize (organise / organize; both used in Britain), -ence / -ense (licence noun / license), programme / program, grey / gray.

Such as

Such as introduces one or more examples: I like fruit such as apples and bananas. Big cities, such as London or Paris, are expensive. Don't use etc. after a list with such as (it is already incomplete). Compare like, which is more informal: fruit like apples.
