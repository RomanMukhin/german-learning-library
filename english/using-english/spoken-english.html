Pronunciation

English spelling doesn't show pronunciation reliably, so learn the sound of each new word. Key features of natural speech:

  • word stress: one syllable is stronger: PHOtograph, phoTOgrapher, photoGRAphic
  • weak forms: small grammar words are reduced in normal speech: to /tə/, and /ən/, can /kən/, of /əv/
  • schwa /ə/: the most common English vowel, in unstressed syllables: about, sofa
  • linking: words run together: an apple → /əˈnæpl/
  • sentence stress: content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives) are stressed, grammar words aren't

Intonation

Intonation is the rise and fall of the voice. It shows whether we are finished, asking, or uncertain.

  • falling ↘: statements, wh-questions, commands, certainty — I live in ↘London. Where do you ↘live?
  • rising ↗: yes/no questions, surprise, checking, lists not finished — Are you ↗ready? ↗Really?
  • fall-rise ↘↗: something unsaid, reservations, politeness — Well, it's ↘↗possible… (but I doubt it)

Interjections (ouch, hooray)

Interjections are short words that express feelings: ouch (pain), oops (small mistake), hooray (joy), ugh / yuck (disgust), wow (surprise, admiration), phew (relief), er / um (hesitation), shh (be quiet), aha (understanding), hmm (thinking, doubt). They are typical of speech and informal writing.

Tags

Uses and form

Tags are short structures added to the end of a sentence to check, invite agreement, or emphasise.

Question tags

Form: auxiliary (or be) + pronoun, usually opposite in polarity to the main clause:

  • Type 1 (positive + negative tag): It's cold, isn't it? You live here, don't you?
  • Type 2 (negative + positive tag): You aren't busy, are you? She didn't come, did she?

Falling intonation = I expect agreement; rising = a real question. Note: I'm late, aren't I? Let's go, shall we? Nobody called, did they?

Same-polarity tags (positive + positive) show interest or surprise: So you're leaving, are you?

Imperative tags

Sit down, will you? Pass the salt, would you? Don't forget, will you?

Statement tags

They repeat or add emphasis: He's a good player, Tom is. I'm hungry, I am. (informal British)

Universal tags

Informal speech uses invariable tags: right?, yeah?, eh? (Br): You're coming, right?

Chunks

Chunks are groups of words that speakers store and use as single units: at the end of the day, I don't know if…, Do you know what I mean?, as far as I know, the thing is…

In speaking and writing

In speech, chunks give us time and sound fluent: I don't know if you remember… You know the shop next to the bank? In writing, fixed chunks organise text: on the other hand, in addition to, as a result.

Chunks as frames

Some chunks are frames with a slot: the ___ thing is…, It's not that ___, it's just that ___.

Ellipsis

Ellipsis means leaving out words that are understood.

Textual ellipsis

Words are left out because they appear earlier in the text: "Are you coming?" "Yes, I am (coming)." She can swim, but I can't (swim).

Situational ellipsis

In informal speech, words that are clear from the situation are left out:

  • subject pronouns: (I) Hope you're well. (It) Sounds good.
  • subject + auxiliary: (Are you) Ready? (Have you) Seen Tom?
  • articles: (The) Problem is, we're late.
  • questions with tags: (It's a) Nice day, isn't it?
  • fixed expressions: (I'll) See you later. (Thank you.)

Headers and tails

Headers put a topic at the start of a sentence, then refer back to it with a pronoun: That woman in the red coat, she's my teacher. Tails add a noun phrase at the end to clarify or comment: It's very nice, that restaurant. He's a good guy, Tom. Both are typical of informal spoken English.

Hyperbole

Hyperbole is deliberate exaggeration for effect: I've told you a million times! I'm starving. This bag weighs a ton. I nearly died laughing. It is common in speech and not meant literally.

Vague expressions

When we don't know the name

thing, thingy, stuff, whatsit, whatsername: Pass me that thing.

Sounding less factual

sort of, kind of, about, around, -ish, or so, more or less: It's kind of blue. We'll be there around sixish.

Groups and categories

and things (like that), and stuff, and so on, or something, or whatever, and everything: We had sandwiches and stuff.

When vague language is impolite

Too much vagueness can sound careless or unclear in formal writing or professional situations. Use precise words there.

Downtoners

Downtoners reduce the force of what we say: a bit, a little, slightly, rather, fairly, somewhat, kind of, sort of. It's a bit late. I'm slightly worried. They make complaints and criticism sound more polite.

Hedges (just)

Hedges make what we say less direct or less certain, so that it sounds polite or careful.

  • tense and aspect: I was wondering if… / I wanted to ask…
  • modals: It might be a problem. That could be right.
  • vague language: just, sort of, a bit: I just need a minute.
  • verbs: I feel / think / guess / suppose it's too expensive. It seems that…
  • academic writing: The results suggest that…, This may indicate…, It appears that…, to some extent

Substitution

Purpose

Substitution replaces a word or phrase with a shorter one to avoid repetition.

Substituting with do and so

  • do / does / did: "She likes jazz." "So do I." I don't play tennis, but my sister does.
  • do so (formal): He promised to help, and he did so.
  • so after think, hope, expect, suppose, be afraid: "Is it raining?" "I think so."

Substitution for nouns: one, some, ones, that, those

  • I need a pen. Have you got one? / I like the red ones.
  • "Any milk?" "There's some in the fridge."
  • indefinite quantifiers: little, all, both, neither, many: I invited them, but neither came.
  • that / those in formal comparisons: The population of Tokyo is larger than that of London.

All right and alright

All right is the standard spelling; alright is common, especially in informal writing, but some readers see it as incorrect.

  • adjective: Are you all right? (OK, not hurt) / The food was all right. (acceptable)
  • adverb: Did you sleep all right?
  • discourse marker: All right, let's begin. (agreement, starting) / "Can you help?" "All right."

Please and thank you

Please

Please makes requests polite: Can I have a coffee, please? Please sit down. It goes at the start, end, or before the verb: Could you please wait? Yes, please. (accepting an offer) As a verb, please means "make happy": It's hard to please him.

Thank you and thanks

Thank you is neutral; thanks is informal; thanks a lot / thanks very much / cheers (Br informal). Thank you for + noun / -ing: Thank you for helping me. Thank-you as a noun: a thank-you card.

Replying to thanks

You're welcome. Not at all. (Br) No problem. That's OK / all right. My pleasure. Don't mention it.

Thanks to

Thanks to = because of (good or ironic): Thanks to your help, we finished early.

Here and there

Meanings

Here = this place, near the speaker; there = that place, further away. They match this/that, come/go and bring/take: Bring it here. Take it there.

After prepositions and at the front

from here, up there, near here, in here. At the front, a noun subject comes after the verb, a pronoun before: Here comes the bus! Here it comes! There goes my train.

Fixed phrases

  • Here you are / Here you go (giving something)
  • There you are (giving, or "I told you so")
  • Here it is! / There he is! (finding)
  • Hello there! (friendly greeting)

Just

  • "simply, absolutely": It's just wonderful. I just don't understand.
  • "exactly": That's just what I wanted. just like her mother
  • "only": It's just a scratch. Just a moment.
  • time: I've just arrived. (very recently) I'm just leaving. (now) just before / after
  • emphasis: Just look at this!
  • softening: Could I just ask a question?

Kind of and sort of

In informal speech, kind of and sort of (written kinda / sorta in very informal texts) make what we say less precise or less direct: It's kind of strange. I sort of understand. They can also be literal: What kind of music?

Oh

As an interjection and discourse marker

Oh shows surprise, understanding or a sudden thought: Oh, I forgot! Oh, that's a shame. Oh, by the way…

Common combinations

  • oh yes / oh no — remembering, emphasising, disappointment
  • oh right — I understand now
  • oh yeah? — interest or doubt
  • oh well — accepting something you can't change
  • oh dear — mild worry or sympathy

So and not with expect, hope, think, etc.

After think, believe, expect, suppose, guess, hope, be afraid, use so to replace a positive clause and not for a negative one:

  • "Will it rain?" "I think so." / "I hope not." / "I'm afraid so."
  • With think, believe, expect the negative is usually I don't think so, rather than I think not (formal).

Typical errors

  • I think it. / I hope it. → I think so. / I hope so.

So

So + adjective/adverb, so much, so many

It's so difficult. She speaks so slowly. so much money, so many people.

So as a substitute

"Is she coming?" "I think so." If so, … It's expensive, more so than I expected. He said so.

So am I, so do I, neither do I

Agreeing with positive statements: "I'm tired." "So am I." "I love it." "So do I." With negatives: "I can't swim." "Neither / Nor can I."

In exclamations

It was so good! I'm so sorry!

As a conjunction and with that-clauses

  • result: It was late, so we went home.
  • so … that: It was so cold that the river froze.
  • purpose: so (that): I left early so (that) I wouldn't miss the bus.

As a discourse marker

So starts a topic, sums up, or asks for more: So, what did you do at the weekend? So that's it.

Yes

Yes is the normal answer agreeing or saying something is true. In informal speech: yeah, yep, uh-huh, sure. When answering negative questions, yes agrees with the positive fact: "Aren't you coming?" "Yes, I am." / "No, I'm not." Yes with rising intonation can mean "Can I help you?" or "Go on".

Anyway

  • "in spite of that": It was raining, but we went out anyway.
  • returning to the main topic after an interruption: Anyway, as I was saying…
  • moving on in a story: Anyway, we finally got there.
  • closing a conversation: Anyway, I'd better go.
  • "at least, more accurately": She's leaving — anyway, that's what she told me.

Discourse markers (so, right, okay)

Discourse markers are words and phrases that organise and manage conversation.

Purpose Markers
starting Right, … / Okay, … / So, … / Well, …
ending Anyway, … / Right then. / Okay, that's it.
changing topic By the way, … / Anyway, … / Talking of …
ordering First(ly), … / Then, … / Finally, …
reformulating I mean, … / In other words, … / Or rather, …
shared knowledge You know, … / You see, … / As you know, …
responding, showing interest Really? / Right. / Absolutely. / I see. / Exactly. / Uh-huh.

In fact

In fact adds information that is more exact, surprising or that contrasts with what was said: I don't mind. In fact, I'd love to help. He said he'd be late, but in fact he arrived early. It is similar to actually and as a matter of fact.

Okay, OK

  • discourse marker: Okay, let's start. / OK, see you tomorrow.
  • agreeing: "Shall we go?" "OK."
  • checking understanding: Turn left at the church, OK?
  • adjective: The film was OK (acceptable, not great). Are you OK?
  • adverb: She's doing OK.

Both spellings are correct; OK is more common.

Well

As a discourse marker

Well gives thinking time, softens disagreement, or shows a change: Well, I'm not sure. Well, actually, I disagree. Well, well! (surprise) Oh well. (acceptance)

As an adverb, adjective, and well vs good

  • adverb: She sings well. (good is the adjective: She's a good singer.)
  • adjective (health): I'm not well. Get well soon.
  • well + participle: a well-known writer, well done

You know

You know is a very common marker in informal speech. It checks shared knowledge, gives thinking time, or emphasises: I met him at, you know, that café near the station. You know what? I'm going to quit. Too much you know sounds unconfident in formal speaking.

You see

You see introduces an explanation, especially of something the listener might not know: I can't come tomorrow. You see, my mother is ill. It is softer than because.
