## Word order and focus

### Typical word order

English has fixed word order in statements: **subject – verb – object – (manner – place – time)**: *She **bought** a car in London last year.* Questions and negatives use auxiliaries before the subject: ***Did** she buy…?* *She **didn't** buy…*

### Untypical word order

We can change the normal order to focus on information, make contrasts or link to the previous sentence. The end of a clause is usually the place for new, important information (**end focus**).

### Fronting

Moving an element to the front gives it emphasis or contrast:

» object fronting: ***This book** I really liked; **that one** I hated.*
» adjunct fronting: ***In the corner** sat an old man.* ***Last summer** we went to Greece.*

### Headers and tails

In speech, a noun phrase can be placed before (header) or after (tail) the clause: ***My brother**, he lives in Leeds.* *It's lovely, **that dress**.*

## Word order: structures

### Indirect object or prepositional complement

*I gave **Anna** the book* (focus on the book) vs *I gave the book **to Anna*** (focus on Anna). The new, important information goes at the end.

### Active and passive voice

The passive allows us to put the receiver first and keep the topic: *The museum opened in 1990. **It was designed by** a Danish architect.*

### Cleft sentences and focus structures

» ***It*-cleft**: ***It was** Tom **who** broke it.*
» ***Wh*-cleft**: ***What** I need **is** a holiday.*
» ***The thing (is)***: ***The thing is**, I don't have time.* ***The one thing** I hate is waiting.*
» ***Something***: ***Something** I've always wanted to do is skydive.*
» ***There***: ***There's** someone at the door.* (new information in end position)

## Cleft sentences (It was in June we got married.)

Cleft sentences divide one idea into two parts to focus on one element.

### It-cleft sentences

*It + be + focus + that/who + rest*: *Anna called me yesterday.* →

» ***It was Anna who** called me yesterday.* (not someone else)
» ***It was yesterday that** Anna called me.* (not another day)
» ***It was in June** (that) we got married.*

### Wh-cleft sentences (What I need is a holiday)

*What + clause + be + focus*: ***What I need is** a holiday.* ***What happened was** that the car broke down.* ***What she did was** call the police.* (*All*, *The place where*, *The reason why* work similarly: ***All I want is** some sleep.*)

## Fronting

Fronting puts something at the start of a clause that normally comes later, for emphasis or to link with previous text:

» ***Such** was the noise that nobody could sleep.*
» ***Strange** as it may seem, I enjoyed it.*
» ***Never** have I seen such a mess.* (with inversion)
» ***Down** came the rain.* ***Here** comes the bus.* (adverb of place + verb + noun subject)

Fronting is more common in writing and in emphatic speech.

## Inversion

### When does inversion happen?

Inversion means the verb (or auxiliary) comes before the subject, as in questions. In statements it happens after certain words at the beginning of a clause, mainly in formal or literary style.

### Negative and limiting adverbs

After *never, rarely, seldom, hardly, scarcely, little, at no time, under no circumstances, only then, only when*: ***Never had** I felt so happy.* ***Little did** she know…* ***Under no circumstances should** you open this door.* ***Only then did** I understand.*

### Expressions beginning with not

***Not only** did he lie, but he also stole.* ***Not until** later **did** I realise.* ***Not once did** she complain.*

### Conditionals and so / neither

***Had** I known… **Should** you need… **Were** I you…* / *"I'm tired." "**So am I**."* *"I can't." "**Neither can I**."*

### Here and there

***Here comes** the train.* ***There goes** the bus.* (but with pronouns: *Here it comes.*)

## No sooner

*No sooner … than* means one thing happened immediately after another. It usually uses the past perfect, and with *no sooner* at the front there is inversion:

» *We had **no sooner** arrived **than** it started to rain.*
» ***No sooner had** we arrived **than** it started to rain.* (formal, emphatic)

Use *than*, not ~~when~~: compare ***Hardly / Scarcely** had we arrived **when**…*

## Not only … but also

*Not only … but (also)* adds emphasis to two linked ideas, the second often surprising:

» *She is **not only** clever **but also** kind.*
» *He **not only** wrote the songs **but also** sang them.*
» ***Not only** is it cheap, **but** it's **also** fast.* (with inversion at the front)

Put *not only* directly before the part it focuses on, and keep the two parts parallel.
