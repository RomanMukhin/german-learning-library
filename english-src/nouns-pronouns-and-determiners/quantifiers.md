## A bit

*A bit* is an informal quantifier and degree word meaning "a little".

» **a bit of** + uncountable noun: *Can I have **a bit of** cake?*
» **a bit** + adjective/adverb: *I'm **a bit** tired.* *Speak **a bit** slower.* *a bit more*
» **a bit** alone as an adverb: *Wait **a bit**.*

***Not a bit*** means "not at all": *I'm **not a bit** surprised.* ***Quite a bit*** means "quite a lot".

## All

### As a determiner

*All* + plural or uncountable noun means "the whole number or amount": ***All** children need love.* ***All** (the) money was spent.*

### All with no article, all of, all without of

» no article for general statements: ***All** cars have brakes.*
» ***all (of) the / my / these*** + noun: ***all (of) the** students*
» ***all of*** + object pronoun (*of* required): ***all of us***, ***all of them***

### All with personal pronouns and as a pronoun

After a pronoun object or subject: *We **all** agree.* *I love you **all**.* Alone as a pronoun, *all* usually means "everything, the only thing": ***All** I want is peace.* Don't say ~~All are here~~; use *Everyone is here*.

### All as an adverb

It means "completely": *She was **all** alone.* *You're **all** wet.* ***Not all*** = some, not every: ***Not all** birds can fly.* ***After all*** = despite expectations, or "remember that": *It didn't rain **after all**.*

## Any

### As a determiner

**Weak** *any* means "an indefinite amount" and is typical in questions and negatives: *Have you got **any** money?* *There aren't **any** eggs.*

**Strong** *any* (stressed) means "it doesn't matter which": *Take **any** seat you like.* *Call me at **any** time.*

### As a pronoun and any of

*"Do you have any cash?" "No, I haven't got **any**."* ***Any of*** + determiner/pronoun: *Have **any of** you seen my keys?*

### Not any and no; any and every

*There **isn't any** milk* = *There's **no** milk.* *Any* (one or other, whichever) vs *every* (all): *You can come **any** day* (choose one) / *I work **every** day* (all days).

### Any with comparatives; typical errors

*Are you feeling **any better**?* *It isn't **any** easier.* Don't use *any* in affirmative statements meaning "some": ~~I bought any apples~~ → *I bought **some** apples.*

## Both

### With nouns and pronouns

*Both* refers to two people or things together: ***Both** (the) children are at school.* ***Both** my parents are teachers.* ***Both of** them / us / you*. After a pronoun object: *I love them **both**.*

### Position

Like *all*, *both* goes after *be* and auxiliaries but before other verbs: *They **are both** tall.* *They have **both** finished.* *They **both** like jazz.*

### Short answers and negatives

*"Tea or coffee?" "**Both**, please."* In negatives, use ***neither (of)*** rather than *both … not*: ***Neither of** them came* (not ~~Both of them didn't come~~).

### Both … and

*Both … and* joins two items: *She speaks **both** French **and** German.* Typical error: ~~both … as well as~~, ~~the both~~.

## Either

### As a determiner and either of

*Either* + singular noun = one or the other of two: *You can park on **either** side.* (= on one side or the other, or sometimes both sides) ***Either of*** + plural noun/pronoun: ***Either of** these dates is fine.*

### As a pronoun

*"Which one?" "**Either**. I don't mind."*

### As an adverb

In negatives, *either* means "also": *I don't like it **either**.* *"I can't swim." "Me **neither**." / "I can't **either**."*

### As a conjunction

***Either** … **or*** gives two alternatives: *We can **either** walk **or** take the bus.*

## Enough

### As a determiner

*Enough* goes **before** nouns: *There's **enough** food.* *We don't have **enough** chairs.*

### As a pronoun

*"More rice?" "No thanks, I've had **enough**."* ***Enough of*** + determiner: ***enough of** the cake*.

### As an adverb

*Enough* goes **after** adjectives and adverbs: *Is it **warm enough**?* *You didn't run **fast enough**.* Not: ~~enough warm~~. It is often followed by *for* or a *to*-infinitive: *She's **old enough to** drive.*

## Least, the least, at least

*Least* is the superlative of *little* (opposite of *most*). ***The least*** + uncountable noun: *Who has **the least** money?* ***The least*** + adjective: *the **least** expensive option*. With plurals, formal English prefers *the fewest*.

### At least

*At least* means "not less than" or "anyway, in spite of problems": *It will cost **at least** £50.* *It rained, but **at least** we had a tent.*

## Less

*Less* is the comparative of *little* (opposite of *more*).

» **less** + uncountable noun: ***less** time, **less** sugar*
» **less** + adjective/adverb: ***less** expensive*, ***less** often*
» **less of** + determiner: ***less of** the cake*
» as a pronoun: *I earn **less** than you.*
» ***less and less***: *I see him **less and less**.*

With plural nouns, careful English uses *fewer*: ***fewer** people*.

## Little, a little, few, a few

| | Countable (plural) | Uncountable |
|---|---|---|
| some, a small amount (positive) | **a few** friends | **a little** time |
| not much / not many (negative) | **few** friends | **little** time |

» I have **a few** friends here, so I'm happy.
» I have **few** friends here, so I'm lonely.

*Little* and *few* without *a* are formal; in speech we often say *not much / not many*. Both can be pronouns and take *of*: ***a few of** them*. ***Quite a few*** = quite a lot.

### A little as an adverb and modifier

*I slept **a little**.* *I'm **a little** tired.* *a **little** more*, *a **little** earlier*.

### Little or small?

*Little* as an adjective often adds feeling (affection, sympathy): *a sweet **little** house*. *Small* is neutral and is used with *very*, comparatives and after a verb: *The room is **small**.* *a **smaller** room*.

## Lots, a lot, plenty

» ***a lot of / lots of*** + plural or uncountable noun: ***a lot of** people*, ***lots of** time* (*lots of* is more informal)
» without a noun: *"Do you have any friends?" "Yes, **lots**."* *I've learnt **a lot**.*
» ***plenty of*** = more than enough: *Don't hurry, we've got **plenty of** time.*

### Formal styles

In formal writing, prefer ***a great / good deal of*** (uncountable) or ***a large number of*** (plural): *a great deal of effort*, *a large number of students*.

## Many

*Many* goes with plural countable nouns. Like *much*, it is most common in **questions and negatives**: *How **many** brothers have you got?* *There weren't **many** people.* In affirmatives it is normal after *so, too, as* and in formal writing: ***Many** experts believe…* In everyday affirmatives, *a lot of* is more natural.

## More

*More* is the comparative of *much* and *many*.

» with nouns: ***more** money, **more** people*
» with adjectives and adverbs: ***more** interesting*, ***more** quickly*
» ***more of*** + determiner: *Can I have **more of** that soup?*
» as a pronoun: *Would you like **more**?*
» ***more and more***: *It's getting **more and more** expensive.*
» after numbers and quantity words: ***two more** days*, ***some more** tea*, ***any more** questions*

### More or longer?

For time, use *longer*, not *more*: *I can't wait any **longer**.* Not: ~~any more~~ in that meaning.

## Most, the most, mostly

### Most

*Most* (no *the*) = the majority: ***Most** people like music.* ***Most of the / my*** + noun or ***most of*** + pronoun: ***most of the** time*, ***most of** them*. Not: ~~the most people~~, ~~most of people~~.

### The most

*The most* is the superlative: ***the most** beautiful*, *Who ate **the most**?*, *She earns **the most** money*. With verbs: *What I enjoyed **the most** was…*

### Mostly

*Mostly* is an adverb = mainly, usually: *The audience were **mostly** students.* *I **mostly** work from home.*

## Much, many, a lot of, lots of: quantifiers

### Much and many with a noun

| | Uncountable | Plural countable |
|---|---|---|
| questions / negatives | much | many |
| affirmatives (informal) | a lot of / lots of | a lot of / lots of |

» How **much** time do we have? / I don't have **many** books.
» I've got **a lot of** work. Not: ~~I have much work~~ (sounds unnatural)

### Much of, many of; this much

***Much of*** / ***many of*** + determiner: ***many of** my friends*. *This much, that much* show an amount with a gesture: *I need about **this much**.*

### Without a noun

*"Did you eat much?" "Not **much**."* *I've got **lots** / **a lot**.* (no *of* when no noun follows)

### Much with comparatives

***much** older*, ***much** faster*, ***much** more interesting*

### Too much, so much, as much as

***too much** noise*, ***too many** cars*, ***so much** fun*, ***as many as** you like*

### Typical errors

» ~~a lot of~~ without a noun: ~~I like it a lot of~~ → *I like it **a lot***
» ~~much people~~ → ***many** people*

## No, none and none of

### No

*No* is a determiner (= *not any*) before singular, plural and uncountable nouns: *There's **no** bus today.* *I have **no** idea.* *No* + noun is stronger than *not any*.

### None

*None* is a pronoun (= not one, not any): *"How many children do they have?" "**None**."*

### None of

***None of*** + determiner + noun or pronoun: ***None of** my friends came.* ***None of** them **knows / know***. Singular verbs are more formal, plural verbs more common in speech.

### Typical error

» Don't add another negative: ~~None of them didn't come~~ → ***None of** them came.*

## Plenty

### Plenty as a pronoun

*"Do we need more chairs?" "No, there are **plenty**."* (= more than enough)

### Plenty of as a quantifier

***plenty of*** + plural or uncountable noun: ***plenty of** time*, ***plenty of** opportunities*. It is mostly used in affirmative sentences.

### Typical error

» ~~plenty people~~ → ***plenty of** people*; ~~plenty of money is not enough~~ (plenty already means more than enough).

## Some

### As a determiner

**Weak** *some* /səm/ means "an unspecified amount" with plural and uncountable nouns: *I bought **some** apples.* *Would you like **some** tea?*

**Strong** *some* /sʌm/ contrasts with others, or means "a certain" or "large": ***Some** people like it, others don't.* ***Some** man called.* *It took **some** time.*

*Some* + number = "about": ***some** 200 people*.

### Leaving out some

For general statements, no determiner is used: *I like **apples**.* (not ~~some apples~~ for "in general")

### As a pronoun and some of

*"Is there any milk?" "Yes, there's **some** in the fridge."* ***Some of*** + determiner/pronoun: ***some of** my friends*, ***some of** it*.

### Typical errors

» ~~Some of people~~ → ***Some** people* / ***Some of the** people*

## Some and any

*Some* is typical in **affirmative** sentences, **offers** and **requests** (we expect "yes"). *Any* is typical in **negatives** and **open questions**.

» There's **some** bread on the table.
» Would you like **some** coffee? / Could I have **some** water? (offer / request)
» Is there **any** bread left? / There isn't **any** bread.

*Any* is also used in affirmatives with the "it doesn't matter which" meaning, and after *if*: ***If** you have **any** problems, call me.*
