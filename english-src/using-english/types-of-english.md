## British and American English

The grammar of British and American English is almost the same. The main differences:

### Verbs

| | British | American |
|---|---|---|
| possession | *Have you **got** a car?* | *Do you **have** a car?* |
| *get* participle | got | gotten (*He's **gotten** better.*) |
| irregular *-t* forms | burnt, learnt, dreamt (also *-ed*) | burned, learned, dreamed |
| *fit* past | fitted | fit |
| *shall* | *Shall I open the window?* | *Should I…?* |
| substitute *do* | *"Will you come?" "I might **do**."* | *"I might."* |

### Tenses

British English uses the **present perfect** for recent events with *just, already, yet*: *I've **just** eaten.* American English often uses the past simple: *I **just** ate.*

### Prepositions and other differences

» ***at** the weekend* (Br) / ***on** the weekend* (US)
» ***in** a team* (Br) / ***on** a team* (US)
» *Monday **to** Friday* (Br) / *Monday **through** Friday* (US)
» *different from / to* (Br) / *different from / than* (US)
» collective nouns: *The team **are** winning* (Br, possible) / *The team **is** winning* (US)
» spelling: *colour/color, centre/center, organise/organize, travelled/traveled*
» vocabulary: *flat/apartment, lift/elevator, holiday/vacation, mobile/cell phone, pavement/sidewalk*

## Dialect

A dialect is the variety of a language used by a particular region or social group, with its own words, grammar and pronunciation. Examples in British dialects: *youse* (plural *you*), *I were* (northern England), *wee* (= small, Scotland). Dialect is different from **accent**, which is only pronunciation. Standard English is the dialect used in education and most writing.

## Double negatives and usage

In standard English, two negatives in one clause cancel each other out, so we use only one: ~~I didn't see nobody~~ → *I didn't see **anybody*** / *I saw **nobody***. Double negatives are common in some dialects and songs, but are considered non-standard.

### Double negation with adjectives and adverbs

Using *not* with a negative-prefix word is standard and creates a careful, understated meaning: *The result was **not unexpected*** (= somewhat expected). *She's **not unattractive***.

## Formal and informal language

| Informal | Formal |
|---|---|
| contractions: *I'm, don't* | full forms: *I am, do not* |
| phrasal verbs: *find out, put off* | single verbs: *discover, postpone* |
| *get*: *get better, get a job* | *improve, obtain a position* |
| active, personal: *We think…* | passive, impersonal: *It is thought…* |
| vague words: *stuff, thing, a lot of* | precise words: *material, aspect, a great deal of* |
| *Thanks!* / *Hi* / *Bye* | *Thank you.* / *Dear…* / *Yours sincerely* |

Most everyday English is neutral, between the two.

## Newspaper headlines

Headlines save space and attract attention, so they have a special grammar:

» articles and *be* are left out: *PRIME MINISTER IN HOSPITAL*
» present simple for past events: *PRINCE MARRIES ACTRESS*
» *to*-infinitive for the future: *PM TO VISIT INDIA*
» past participle for passive: *TWO KILLED IN CRASH*
» noun strings: *CITY HALL FIRE ALARM DRAMA*
» short words: *bid* (attempt), *row* (argument), *quit* (resign), *axe* (cut)

## Register

Register is the variety of language suited to a particular situation, purpose and relationship. We choose words and grammar according to the **context** (a job interview vs a chat with friends), the **topic** (medicine, law, sport) and the **medium** (speech, email, report). For example, *kids* (informal), *children* (neutral), *minors* (legal).

## Slang

Slang is very informal language, often used by a particular group (young people, a profession) to show belonging. It changes quickly: *cool, awesome, gutted* (Br, very disappointed), *skint* (Br, without money), *quid* (Br, a pound). Avoid slang in formal speech and writing, and be careful using it as a learner; it can sound unnatural.

## Standard and non-standard language

Standard English is the variety used in education, news, official documents and most writing. Non-standard forms are used in some dialects and informal speech, for example *ain't* (= isn't / haven't), *we was*, *I done it*, *them books*. They are not "wrong" in those communities, but in exams and formal contexts use standard forms.

## Swearing and taboo expressions

Taboo words relate to religion, the body, sex and bodily functions. They can be very offensive, and the degree of offence depends on the situation and the listener. Learners should recognise them but use them carefully, if at all. Milder alternatives exist: *Oh my gosh!* (for *Oh my God!*), *Damn!* (mild), *freaking* (mild intensifier).

## Sexist language

### He, she and they

Using *he* for a person of unknown sex is now considered sexist. Use **they**: *Every student must bring **their** own book.* Or make the sentence plural: *Students must bring **their** books.*

### -man, -master, -woman, -ess

Many job words have neutral forms: *chairperson / chair*, *firefighter*, *police officer*, *flight attendant*, *spokesperson*, *headteacher*. *-ess* forms (*actress, waitress, manageress*) are less common now; many women prefer *actor*, *server*, *manager*.

### Man as a word for people

Avoid *man* and *mankind* for all people: use *people, humans, humanity, humankind*. *Man-made* → *artificial, synthetic*; *manpower* → *staff, workforce*.
