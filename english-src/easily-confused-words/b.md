## Been or gone?

Both are past participles of *go*. **Been** means someone went and came back; **gone** means they went and are still there (or on the way).

» She's **been** to Paris. (she visited and returned)
» She's **gone** to Paris. (she's there now)

## Begin or start?

Usually both are possible: *It **began / started** to rain.* Only **start** is used for machines and for beginning a journey or business: *The car won't **start**.* *They **started** a company.* *Begin* is slightly more formal.

## Beside or besides?

**Beside** = next to: *Sit **beside** me.* **Besides** = in addition to, or "also" as a linking adverb: ***Besides** English, she speaks Arabic.* *I'm too tired. **Besides**, it's late.*

## Between or among?

**Between** is used for two clearly separate things or people, or for a clear list of individuals. **Among** is used for a group seen as a mass.

» The house is **between** the bank and the school.
» She was **among** friends. / a village **among** the hills

## Born or borne?

**Born** is used only in *be born*: *I **was born** in 1995.* **Borne** is the normal past participle of *bear* (carry, tolerate, give birth to): *She has **borne** three children.* *The costs will be **borne** by the company.*

## Bring, take and fetch

» **bring** = move something towards the speaker or listener: ***Bring** your photos when you come.*
» **take** = move something away from here: ***Take** an umbrella with you.*
» **fetch** = go somewhere, get something and bring it back (mostly British): *Could you **fetch** my glasses from the kitchen?*

## Can, could or may?

For **ability**, use *can* (present) and *could* (past): *She **can** swim.* For **permission**, *can* is informal, *could* is more polite, *may* is formal: ***Can / Could / May** I sit here?* For **possibility**, *may*, *might* and *could* are used: *It **may** rain later.*

## Classic or classical?

**Classic** = typical, or of very high quality and lasting value: *a **classic** mistake*, *a **classic** film*. **Classical** refers to ancient Greece and Rome, or to serious traditional music: ***classical** music*, ***classical** architecture*.

## Come or go?

**Come** = move towards the speaker or listener (or where they will be); **go** = move away.

» **Come** here! / Can I **come** with you?
» Let's **go** home. / I'm **going** to the shop.

## Consider or regard?

Both can mean "think of as". **Regard** needs **as**: *I **regard** her **as** my best friend.* **Consider** doesn't: *I **consider** her (to be) my best friend.* *Consider* also means "think about carefully": *We're **considering** moving.*

## Consist, comprise or compose?

» **consist of**: *The team **consists of** ten people.* (never passive)
» **comprise** (formal, no *of*): *The team **comprises** ten people.*
» **be composed of**: *The team **is composed of** ten people.*

## Content or contents?

**Contents** (plural) = the things inside something, or the list of chapters: *the **contents** of the box*. **Content** (uncountable) = the subject matter or ideas of a book, speech or website: *The **content** of the course is excellent.* *Content* /kənˈtent/ as an adjective means "satisfied".

## Different from, different to or different than?

**Different from** is the most common and accepted everywhere. **Different to** is common in British English; **different than** in American English.

» Her style is **different from** mine.

## Do or make?

**Make** is used for creating or producing something; **do** for activities, work and jobs in general.

| do | make |
|---|---|
| homework, the housework, the washing-up | a cake, a mistake, a decision |
| a job, business, your best | money, a noise, a phone call |
| exercise, someone a favour | friends, plans, progress, an effort |

## Down, downwards or downward?

**Down** is the usual adverb and preposition: *walk **down** the stairs*. **Downwards** (British) and **downward** (American) are adverbs meaning "towards a lower position": *The road slopes **downwards***. **Downward** is also the adjective: *a **downward** trend*.

## During or for?

**During** tells us *when* something happens; **for** tells us *how long* it lasts.

» I fell asleep **during** the film.
» I slept **for** two hours. Not: ~~during two hours~~

## Each or every?

Both are used with singular nouns. **Each** focuses on individuals and can refer to two; **every** sees the group as a whole and needs at least three.

» She had a bag in **each** hand. (two)
» **Every** student passed. (all of them)

**Each** can be used alone or with *of*: ***each of** them*; *every* can't: ~~every of them~~ → ***every one of** them*.

## East or eastern; north or northern?

**North, south, east, west** are used with names of exact areas and for winds or directions: ***North** America*, *the **east** coast*. **Northern, southern, eastern, western** describe a general area: ***northern** Europe*, *the **western** part of the country*.

## Economic or economical?

**Economic** relates to the economy and money: ***economic** growth*. **Economical** means not wasting money or resources: *an **economical** car*.

## Efficient or effective?

**Effective** = it works, it produces the result you want: *an **effective** treatment*. **Efficient** = it works well without wasting time, money or energy: *an **efficient** engine*.

## Elder, eldest or older, oldest?

**Elder** and **eldest** are used for people in a family, before a noun or as *the elder*: *my **elder** sister*, *the **eldest** son*. They don't go with *than*: *She's **older than** me*, not ~~elder than~~. **Older** and **oldest** are used for everything.

## End or finish?

Often both are possible: *The film **ended / finished** at ten.* **Finish** is used when something is completed: *Have you **finished** your homework?* It is followed by *-ing*: ***finish reading***. **End** is used for the final point of something, or to stop something: *They **ended** their relationship.*
