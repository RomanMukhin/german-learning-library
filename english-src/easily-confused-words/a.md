---
title: A: above to while
sub: Confusing words beginning with A, from above/over and advice/advise to as/like and as/when/while.
order: 1
---
## Above or over?

Both mean "higher than". Use **above** when one thing is not directly on top or when talking about levels and measurements; use **over** when something covers or moves across, or touches.

» The picture hangs **above** the fireplace.
» She put a blanket **over** the baby. (covering)
» The plane flew **over** the city. (movement across)
» 10 degrees **above** zero / children **over** 12 (more than)

## Across, over or through?

**Across** = from one side to the other of a flat area or line: *walk **across** the road*. **Over** = across something high, or from one side to the other going up and down: *climb **over** the wall*. **Through** = inside something with sides, from one end to the other: *drive **through** the tunnel*, *walk **through** the forest*.

## Advice or advise?

**Advice** /ədˈvaɪs/ is an uncountable noun; **advise** /ədˈvaɪz/ is the verb.

» Can you give me some **advice**? Not: ~~an advice~~, ~~advices~~ → *a piece of advice*
» I **advise** you to see a doctor.

## Affect or effect?

**Affect** is usually a verb meaning "influence". **Effect** is usually a noun meaning "result".

» The weather **affects** my mood.
» The weather has an **effect** on my mood.

*Effect* as a verb (formal) means "bring about": *to **effect** change*.

## All or every?

**Every** + singular countable noun looks at each member of a group; **all** + plural or uncountable noun looks at the group as a whole.

» **Every** student has a laptop. / **All** (the) students have laptops.
» **all** day (from start to end) / **every** day (each day)

## All or whole?

**Whole** goes between a determiner and a singular noun; **all** goes before the determiner.

» **the whole** day = **all the** day (*all day* is more common)
» **my whole** life / **all my** life

With uncountable nouns use *all*: ***all** the money*, not ~~the whole money~~.

## Allow, permit or let?

All mean "give permission". **Let** is the most informal and is followed by object + **bare infinitive**; **allow** and **permit** (formal) take object + **to**-infinitive.

» My parents **let** me stay out late.
» My parents **allowed** me **to** stay out late.
» Smoking is not **permitted**.

*Let* is not normally used in the passive: ~~I was let to go~~ → *I was **allowed to** go*.

## Almost or nearly?

Often both are possible: ***almost / nearly** finished*. Only **almost** is used before negative words like *no, nobody, never, nothing* and with comparisons such as *almost like*:

» **Almost** nobody came. Not: ~~nearly nobody~~
» **Very nearly** and **not nearly** (= not at all) use *nearly*: *It's **not nearly** enough.*

## Alone, lonely or lonesome?

**Alone** means "without other people" and is neutral; it comes after a verb. **Lonely** means "unhappy because you are alone". **Lonesome** is American English for *lonely*.

» I live **alone**, but I'm never **lonely**.
» a **lonely** old man / a **lonely** village (far from other places)

## Along or alongside?

**Along** = following the length of something: *walk **along** the river*. **Alongside** = next to, beside, or working together with: *The boat was **alongside** the ship.* *She worked **alongside** her father.*

## Already, still or yet?

» **Already** = sooner than expected: *I've **already** finished.*
» **Still** = continuing, not finished: *I'm **still** working.*
» **Yet** = up to now, in questions and negatives, at the end: *Have you finished **yet**? I haven't finished **yet**.*

## Also, as well or too?

All mean "in addition". **Also** usually goes in mid position; **as well** and **too** go at the end. *As well* and *too* are more common in speech.

» She **also** speaks French. / She speaks French **as well / too**.

In negatives use **either**: *She doesn't speak French **either***.

## Alternate(ly) or alternative(ly)?

**Alternate** means "every other one" or "first one, then the other": *We meet on **alternate** Mondays.* **Alternative** means "another possible choice": *Is there an **alternative** route?* ***Alternatively**, we could take the train.*

## Although or though?

Both mean "despite the fact that". **Though** is more informal and can come at the end of a sentence meaning "however":

» **Although / Though** it was cold, we swam.
» It was cold. We swam, **though**.

*Even though* is stronger; *even although* is not used.

## Altogether or all together?

**Altogether** = completely, or in total: *That's £20 **altogether**.* *I'm not **altogether** sure.* **All together** = everyone or everything in one place or at one time: *Let's sing it **all together**.*

## Amount of, number of or quantity of?

» **an amount of** + uncountable noun: *a large **amount of** money*
» **a number of** + plural noun (plural verb): *A **number of** people **were** late.*
» **a quantity of** + either, often for things you can measure: *a small **quantity of** salt*, *large **quantities of** cars*

## Any more or anymore?

As a time adverb meaning "no longer", British English normally writes **any more** and American English **anymore**: *I don't live there **any more***. For "additional amount" always write two words: *Is there **any more** cake?*

## Anyone, anybody or anything?

**Anyone** and **anybody** mean the same (any person); *anybody* is a little more informal. **Anything** refers to things. They are used in questions, negatives, and to mean "it doesn't matter who/what":

» Is **anyone** there? / I didn't see **anybody**.
» **Anyone** can do it. / You can eat **anything**.

## Apart from or except for?

**Apart from** can mean both "except" and "in addition to". **Except for** means only "not including".

» **Apart from / Except for** Tom, everyone came. (Tom didn't)
» **Apart from** football, he plays tennis. (in addition)

## Arise or rise?

**Arise** (*arose, arisen*) means a problem or situation begins: *A problem has **arisen**.* **Rise** (*rose, risen*) means go up: *Prices **rose** by 5%.* *The sun **rises** in the east.* Neither takes an object.

## Around or round?

In British English both are used for movement in a circle or in different directions: *walk **(a)round** the town*. **Around** is more common for "approximately": ***around** 50 people*. American English uses *around* in most cases.

## Arouse or rouse?

**Arouse** means cause a feeling or reaction: *Her behaviour **aroused** suspicion.* **Rouse** means wake someone up or make them active (literary): *He was **roused** from sleep.*

## As or like?

**Like** is a preposition followed by a noun or pronoun and means "similar to". **As** + noun means "in the role of"; **as** + clause means "in the same way that".

» She sings **like** an angel. (similar to)
» He works **as** a teacher. (that is his job)
» Do it **as** I showed you. (clause) — informal: *like I showed you*

## As, because or since?

All give reasons. **Because** is the most common and puts focus on the reason; it often comes second. **As** and **since** suggest the reason is already known, and often come first; they are a little more formal.

» I stayed home **because** I was ill.
» **As / Since** it was raining, we took a taxi.

## As, when or while?

All introduce time clauses. **When** is for a point in time or a general time: ***When** I arrived, she left.* **While** is for a longer action in progress: ***While** I was cooking, the phone rang.* **As** suggests two actions developing together: ***As** I got older, I became calmer.*
