## High or tall?

**Tall** is used for people and for things that are narrow and higher than usual: *a **tall** man*, *a **tall** building*, *a **tall** tree*. **High** is used for mountains, walls and distance from the ground: *a **high** mountain*, *a **high** shelf*. For people, don't use ~~high~~.

## Historic or historical?

**Historic** = very important in history: *a **historic** moment*, *a **historic** building*. **Historical** = connected with the past or history as a subject: ***historical** documents*, *a **historical** novel*.

## House or home?

A **house** is a building. **Home** is the place where you live and feel you belong (it can be a flat). Note: *go **home**, be **at home***, with no *to* and no article.

» They've bought a new **house**.
» I want to go **home**. Not: ~~go to home~~

## How is …? or What is … like?

**How is …?** asks about someone's health or about how something is going right now: *How's your mother? How's the new job?* **What is … like?** asks for a description: *What's your new boss **like**? What was the weather **like**?*

## If or when?

**When** is used for things you are sure will happen; **if** for things that may or may not happen.

» I'll call you **when** I get home. (I will get home)
» I'll call you **if** I have time. (maybe)

## If or whether?

Both introduce indirect yes/no questions: *I don't know **if / whether** she's coming.* Use **whether** (not *if*) after prepositions, before *to*-infinitives, and with *or not* directly after it: *It depends on **whether** it rains.* *I can't decide **whether to** go.*

## Ill or sick?

Both mean "not healthy". **Ill** usually comes after a verb in British English (*She's **ill***); **sick** goes before nouns (*a **sick** child*) and in American English is used everywhere. In British English *be sick* also means "vomit". *Sick of* = tired of: *I'm **sick of** waiting.*

## Imply or infer?

A speaker **implies** something (suggests it without saying it directly); a listener **infers** it (understands it from what was said).

» Are you **implying** that I'm lying?
» I **inferred** from her tone that she was angry.

## In the way or on the way?

**In the way** = blocking someone: *Please move, you're **in the way**.* **On the way** = during a journey, or coming soon: *I'll buy bread **on the way** home.* *The pizza is **on the way**.*

## It's or its?

**It's** = *it is* or *it has*: ***It's** raining.* ***It's** been a long day.* **Its** = belonging to it (no apostrophe): *The dog wagged **its** tail.*

## Late or lately?

**Late** = after the expected time: *The train arrived **late**.* **Lately** = recently, and usually goes with the present perfect: *Have you seen him **lately**?*

## Lay or lie?

**Lay** (*laid, laid*) takes an object: put something down: ***Lay** the baby on the bed.* **Lie** (*lay, lain*) has no object: be in a flat position: *I **lay** on the beach all day.* **Lie** (*lied, lied*) means "say something untrue".

| | Present | Past | Participle |
|---|---|---|---|
| put down | lay | laid | laid |
| be flat | lie | lay | lain |
| say untrue | lie | lied | lied |

## Lend or borrow?

You **lend** something **to** someone (give it for a time); you **borrow** something **from** someone (take it for a time).

» Can you **lend** me £10?
» Can I **borrow** £10 **from** you? Not: ~~Can you borrow me~~

## Less or fewer?

Traditionally, **less** goes with uncountable nouns and **fewer** with plural nouns: ***less** time*, ***fewer** people*. In informal speech many people say *less people*, but in formal writing use *fewer*.

## Look at, see or watch?

» **see** = notice with your eyes, without trying: *I **saw** a fox in the garden.*
» **look at** = direct your eyes on purpose: ***Look at** this photo.*
» **watch** = look at something that moves or changes, for a period: ***watch** TV*, ***watch** a match*

## Low or short?

**Short** is the opposite of *tall* (people) and *long* (length or time): *a **short** man*, *a **short** film*. **Low** is the opposite of *high*: *a **low** wall*, ***low** prices*.

## Man, mankind or people?

**People** is the normal way to talk about human beings in general: ***People** need sleep.* **Man** and **mankind** were used in this way in the past, but many people now prefer neutral words: *humans, humanity, humankind*.

## Maybe or may be?

**Maybe** (one word) is an adverb meaning "perhaps": ***Maybe** she's ill.* **May be** (two words) is the modal verb + *be*: *She **may be** ill.*

## Maybe or perhaps?

They mean the same. **Maybe** is more common in informal speech; **perhaps** is a little more formal and polite: ***Perhaps** you could help me?*

## Nearest or next?

**Nearest** = closest in distance: *Where's the **nearest** bank?* **Next** = the one after this one in order or time: *Get off at the **next** stop.* *I'll see you **next** week.*

## Never or not … ever?

**Never** means "not at any time": *I've **never** been to Rome.* **Not … ever** means the same but is less common and more emphatic: *I **won't ever** forget this.* Don't use *never* with another negative: ~~I don't never~~.

## Nice or sympathetic?

**Nice** = pleasant, kind: *She's really **nice**.* **Sympathetic** = showing that you understand someone's problems and care: *He was very **sympathetic** when my father died.* In some languages a similar word means "nice"; in English it does not.

## No doubt or without doubt?

**No doubt** = probably, I'm fairly sure: ***No doubt** you've heard the news.* **Without (a) doubt** = certainly, definitely: *She is **without doubt** the best player.*

## No or not?

**No** is a determiner before nouns (= *not any*): *There's **no** milk.* **Not** makes verbs, adjectives, adverbs and other words negative: *I'm **not** hungry.* *There is**n't any** milk.* As a reply, use *no*.

## Nowadays, these days or today?

All three mean "at the present time, in contrast with the past". **These days** is the most common in speech. **Today** also means "this day". *Nowadays* is usually written as one word.

» **Nowadays / These days**, most people have a smartphone.

## Open or opened?

**Open** is the adjective (state): *The door is **open***. Not: ~~The door is opened~~ for the state. **Opened** is the past tense / participle of the verb: *She **opened** the door.* *The shop was **opened** in 1990.*

## Opportunity or possibility?

An **opportunity** is a chance to do something you want: *I had the **opportunity** to study abroad.* A **possibility** is something that might happen: *There's a **possibility** of rain.* Don't say ~~I had the possibility to study~~.

## Opposite or in front of?

**Opposite** = facing, on the other side (of a road, table): *She sat **opposite** me.* **In front of** = directly before, in the same direction: *There's a car parked **in front of** my house.*

## Other, others, the other or another?

» **another** + singular noun = one more / a different one: *another cup*
» **other** + plural noun: *other people*
» **the other** = the remaining one(s) of a known group: *the other hand*
» **others** (pronoun, no noun after it): *Some like tea, **others** prefer coffee.*

Not: ~~others people~~, ~~an other~~.

## Out or out of?

**Out of** is a preposition followed by a noun: *She ran **out of** the house.* **Out** is an adverb with no noun after it: *She ran **out**.* In American English *out* alone is sometimes used before *the door* or *the window*.
