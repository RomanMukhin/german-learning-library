## Afraid

### Afraid meaning "feeling fear"

*Afraid* comes after a verb, not before a noun: *The child **was afraid**.* Not: ~~an afraid child~~ (use *a frightened child*). It is followed by **of** + noun/-ing or by a **to**-infinitive:

» She's **afraid of** dogs.
» I'm **afraid of** losing my job. (fear of something that might happen)
» He was **afraid to** ask. (too frightened to do it)

### Afraid: softening

***I'm afraid** (that)* is a polite way to give bad news or say no: ***I'm afraid** we're closed.* *"Is there any cake left?" "**I'm afraid not**."* *"Do we have to pay?" "**I'm afraid so**."*

## Alike

### Alike as an adjective

*Alike* means "similar" and comes after the verb, not before a noun: *The two sisters **are** very **alike**.* Not: ~~two alike sisters~~ (use *two similar sisters*).

### Alike as an adverb

It means "in the same way" or "equally": *The twins dress **alike**.* *The film was popular with adults and children **alike**.*

## Hard

*Hard* is both an adjective and an adverb. As an adjective it means "solid" or "difficult"; as an adverb it means "with a lot of effort or force".

» a **hard** chair / a **hard** question
» She works **hard**. / It's raining **hard**.

*Hardly* is a different word meaning "almost not": *He **hardly** works* (= he does very little work). See [Hardly](#hardly).

## Long

### Time

*Long* as an adverb is used mainly in questions and negatives: *How **long** did you wait?* *I won't be **long**.* In affirmative sentences use *a long time*: *I waited **a long time***, not ~~I waited long~~.

### Distance and length

*How **long** is the river?* *The film is three hours **long**.* For distance we usually say *far*: *How **far** is it?*

### As long as, no longer

***As long as*** means "provided that": *You can borrow it **as long as** you bring it back.* *No longer* means "not any more": *She **no longer** lives here.*

### Typical error

» ~~Since long I live here.~~ → *I've lived here **for a long time**.*

## Only

### Only as an adjective

*Only* before a noun means "single, no others": *She's an **only** child.* *It's the **only** way.*

### Only as an adverb

It means "no more than" or "just": *It **only** costs £5.* *I **only** wanted to help.*

### Position

In speech *only* usually goes in mid position, and stress shows what it refers to. In careful writing, put it next to the word it focuses on:

» **Only** Anna saw the accident. (nobody else)
» Anna **only** saw the accident. (she didn't do anything else)
» Anna saw **only** the end of the film.

At the front of a sentence, *only* + time expression causes inversion: ***Only then did I** understand.*

## Same, similar, identical

### The same as

We use **the** with *same*, and **as** before a noun or pronoun: *Your phone is **the same as** mine.* Not: ~~same like~~, ~~the same that~~ (except before a clause).

### The same + noun + clause

» We went to **the same school**.
» She wore **the same dress as** I did / **that** she wore last year.

### Do the same

*Do the same* means "do the same thing": *She left early, and I **did the same**.*

### Similar and identical

*Similar* means "alike but not exactly the same", and takes **to**: *Your bag is **similar to** mine.* *Identical* means "exactly the same", often with **to**: *The two pictures are **identical**.*

## Likely and unlikely

*Likely* (= probable) is an adjective, not normally an adverb. It is followed by a **to**-infinitive or a **that**-clause:

» It's **likely to** rain later.
» She's **unlikely to** win.
» **It's likely that** prices will rise.

### Typical error

» ~~He will likely come.~~ (common in American English) → British English: *He'**s likely to** come* or *He will **probably** come.*

## As well (as)

### Meaning "in addition"

*As well* goes at the end of a clause: *I like tennis, and I like golf **as well**.* *As well as* is followed by a noun or *-ing*: ***As well as** French, she speaks Italian.* *She sings **as well as playing** the piano.*

### Might as well, may as well

These suggest doing something because there's no better option: *The last bus has gone, so we **might as well** walk.*

### As well, too and also

*As well* and *too* go at the end; *also* usually goes in mid position. *As well* is a little more informal than *also*.

## Even

### Even: meaning and position

*Even* shows that something is surprising or more than expected. It usually goes in mid position, or before the word it focuses on:

» He **even** forgot his own birthday.
» **Even** my grandmother uses a smartphone.

### Even and comparatives

*Even* makes comparatives stronger: *Today is **even hotter** than yesterday.*

### Even though, even if, even so

» ***Even though*** it was raining, we went out. (= although, it really was raining)
» ***Even if*** it rains, we'll go out. (= whether or not it rains)
» *It was expensive. **Even so**, I bought it.* (= despite that)

Don't use *even* alone as a conjunction: ~~Even it was late, we stayed~~.

## Hardly

*Hardly* means "almost not". It does not mean "in a hard way".

» I can **hardly** hear you.
» We **hardly** know each other.

### Hardly any, hardly ever, hardly at all

» There's **hardly any** milk left. (= very little)
» She **hardly ever** goes out. (= almost never)
» I slept **hardly at all**.

*Hardly any* is similar to *very little / very few*, but stronger.

### Word order

*Hardly* is negative, so don't use another negative with it: ~~I can't hardly~~. In formal writing, *hardly … when* can start a sentence with inversion: ***Hardly had** I arrived **when** it began to rain.*

## Hopefully

*Hopefully* is mostly used as a sentence adverb meaning "I hope that": ***Hopefully**, the weather will be good tomorrow.* It can also mean "in a hopeful way": *The dog looked up **hopefully***.

## Surely

*Surely* doesn't mean the same as *certainly*. It shows that the speaker believes something strongly and is surprised that someone might think differently:

» **Surely** you're not going out in that rain!
» You've finished already? **Surely** not!

In American English *surely* can also be a reply meaning "of course". For simple certainty, British English uses *certainly* or *definitely*.

## Too

### Too meaning "more than enough"

*Too* before an adjective or adverb means "more than is good or necessary": *This coffee is **too hot**.* *You're driving **too fast**.*

» It's **too** cold **to** swim. (too + adjective + to-infinitive)
» The box was too heavy **for me to** carry.

### Too much, too many, too few, too little

» **too much** sugar / **too many** people / **too few** chairs / **too little** time

### Much too, far too

To make *too* stronger, use *much*, *far* or *way* (informal): ***far too** expensive*. Not: ~~very too~~.

### Too and very

*Very* is just strong; *too* means it causes a problem: *It's **very** hot, but I'll wear it* / *It's **too** hot to wear*.

### Too meaning "also"

At the end of a clause, *too* means "also": *I'm hungry. — Me **too**.* In negatives use *either*: *I'm not hungry either*, not ~~not hungry too~~.

### Too bad

*Too bad* (informal) means "that's unfortunate", often without real sympathy: *"I missed the bus." "**Too bad**."*

## Ultimately

*Ultimately* means "finally, in the end" or "most importantly, at the most basic level":

» They **ultimately** decided to sell the house.
» **Ultimately**, it's your decision.
